5  Randomized Experiments, Regression Adjustment, and Calibration

NoteLearning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Work in the design-based framework of a completely randomized experiment: compute a randomization distribution, state and prove Neyman’s theorem for the difference in means, explain why the Neyman variance estimator is conservative, and carry out a Fisher randomization test.
  2. Distinguish the finite-population ATE \(\bar\tau_N\) from the superpopulation ATE, and relate the two variances.
  3. State ignorability, identify the ATE and ATT from it, and choose pretreatment adjustment variables.
  4. Construct the regression estimator \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}}\), separate the estimation error from the sampling error, and derive its influence function and variance estimator under a correct linear outcome model.
  5. Explain why, under complete randomization, the regression estimator is design consistent and asymptotically no less precise than the difference in means without any outcome model, and why this guarantee is lost in an observational study.
  6. Write the regression estimator as a calibration-weighted mean, derive the weights by Lagrange multipliers, characterize optimal calibration weights by the KKT conditions, and read the solution as an orthogonal projection.
  7. Recognize stratification as calibration on stratum indicators, state its conditions, and place regression, stratification, and the propensity score as implementations of one identification formula.

5.1 Randomized Experiments

For each unit we observe only one of its two potential outcomes, \(Y_i = Y_i(T_i)\), so the unit-level effect \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) is never observed. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference (Holland 1986). Holland distinguishes two ways around it. The scientific solution measures both outcomes on units that are assumed to be interchangeable, for example identical twins or the same piece of equipment before and after a repair; it rests on a homogeneity assumption that the data cannot check. The statistical solution assigns treatment at random and studies averages. This section develops the statistical solution.

Two kinds of studies differ in who decides the treatment. In a randomized experiment, a known random mechanism decides which units receive \(T_i = 1\); a clinical trial is the standard example. In an observational study, other factors decide \(T_i\): a physician’s judgment, a participant’s choice, an administrative rule. The mechanism is unknown. The rest of this section uses one fact only: in an experiment the assignment mechanism is known, and that knowledge supplies both the identification of the causal effect and the probability distribution used for inference.

Notation. Throughout the design-based parts of this chapter we use the notation of survey sampling. The \(N\) units in the experiment form a finite population \[\mathcal F_N = \bigl\{\bigl(Y_i(1),\, Y_i(0)\bigr) : i = 1, \ldots, N\bigr\},\] treated as fixed numbers. \(N_1\) units are treated and \(N_0 = N - N_1\) are controls, so a sum over \(i = 1, \ldots, N\) is a sum over the population, and a sum over \(\{i : T_i = 1\}\) is a sum over the treated arm. The target is the finite-population average treatment effect \[\bar\tau_N \;=\; \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \bigl\{Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\bigr\} \;=\; \bar Y(1) - \bar Y(0), \qquad \bar Y(t) = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N Y_i(t).\]

5.1.1 A Four-Unit Example

A small example shows every idea of this section with numbers that can be checked by hand.

Example 5.1 (The Science Table) Four units have the potential outcomes in Table 5.1. The table is fixed: it describes the four units before any assignment is made, and nothing in it is random. The finite-population ATE is \(\bar\tau_4 = (3 + 3 + 2 + 2)/4 = 2.5\).

We will never see this table. If unit \(i\) is treated we see \(Y_i(1)\), otherwise \(Y_i(0)\); no unit shows both columns, so no \(\tau_i\) is ever observed. The question is how to learn \(\bar\tau_4 = 2.5\) without seeing a single unit-level effect.

Suppose we treat \(N_1 = 2\) units and leave \(N_0 = 2\) as controls, and estimate \(\bar\tau_4\) by the difference in means (DIM), \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \;=\; \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0 \;=\; \frac1{N_1}\sum_{i:\,T_i=1} Y_i \;-\; \frac1{N_0}\sum_{i:\,T_i=0} Y_i .\] There are \(\binom42 = 6\) ways to choose the treated pair. Table 5.2 lists them. For example, if units 1 and 4 are treated, we observe \(Y_1(1) = 4\), \(Y_4(1) = 8\), \(Y_2(0) = 3\), and \(Y_3(0) = 5\), so \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} = (4 + 8)/2 - (3 + 5)/2 = 2\).

Only one of the six cases will actually happen, and the estimate we report depends on which one. The spread, from \(-0.5\) to \(5.5\) around the target \(2.5\), is the uncertainty that the design creates.

5.1.2 The Completely Randomized Design and the Design-Based View

Definition 5.1 (Completely Randomized Design) A completely randomized design (CRD) chooses \(N_1\) of the \(N\) units for treatment, giving each of the \(\binom{N}{N_1}\) possible assignment vectors \(\mathbf T = (T_1, \ldots, T_N)\) with \(\sum_i T_i = N_1\) the same probability \(1/\binom{N}{N_1}\). Equivalently, the treated arm is a simple random sample without replacement of size \(N_1\) from the \(N\) units, and the control arm is its complement.

Given \(\mathcal F_N\), the only random quantity is \(\mathbf T\). The distribution of an estimator over the \(\binom{N}{N_1}\) equally likely assignments is its randomization distribution.

Example 5.2 (The Randomization Distribution of the Four-Unit Example) Under the CRD with \(N_1 = 2\), each case in Table 5.2 has probability \(1/6\), so \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\) takes the values \(-0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.5\) with probability \(1/6\) each. Its mean is \[\E\bigl(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_4\bigr) = \tfrac16(-0.5 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5.5) = \tfrac{15}{6} = 2.5 = \bar\tau_4,\] so the estimator is unbiased over the randomization. The squared deviations from \(2.5\) are \(9, 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25, 9\), so \[\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_4\bigr) = \tfrac{23}{6} \approx 3.83 .\]

The design-based view. The example made no assumption about how the potential outcomes are generated. The reference distribution is the sampling distribution produced by repeating the given assignment mechanism on the same units. This is the design-based approach: probability statements refer to the physical randomization, not to a model for the outcomes. Readers who know survey sampling will recognize the logic, in which a known sampling design, not a model, supplies the probability.

Randomization serves two purposes (Fisher 1935). First, it makes the treatment and control groups comparable on average, which is the basis of identification (Section 5.1.3). Second, it gives a “reasoned basis” for inference: the probability distribution used for estimation and testing is created by the design, not assumed (Section 5.1.4 and Section 5.1.7).

5.1.3 Identification under Randomization

The four-unit example showed by enumeration that the difference in means averages to the target. The same fact has a population-level form.

Lemma 5.1 (Identification under Complete Randomization) Assume consistency, \(Y_i = T_iY_i(1) + (1 - T_i)Y_i(0)\), and randomization, \[\bigl\{Y(1),\, Y(0)\bigr\} \indep T .\] Then, for \(t \in \{0, 1\}\), \[\E(Y \mid T = t) \;=\; \E\{Y(t)\}, \tag{5.1}\] so the difference in means targets \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E\{Y(1) - Y(0)\}\).

Proof. By consistency, \(\E(Y \mid T = t) = \E\{Y(t) \mid T = t\}\). By randomization, \(Y(t)\) is independent of \(T\), so \(\E\{Y(t) \mid T = t\} = \E\{Y(t)\}\). \(\square\)

Here \(\E\) refers either to a randomly selected unit of the trial population (the empirical distribution of \(\mathcal F_N\)) or to the two-phase superpopulation model of Section 5.1.6. Randomization makes the potential outcomes independent of assignment unconditionally; no covariate adjustment is needed. This is the unconditional counterpart of ignorability (Definition 4.5). In stratified, blocked, or covariate-adaptive designs, where the assignment probability depends on design variables, the natural statement is conditional on those variables. Throughout this chapter, “randomized experiment” means the CRD unless stated otherwise.

WarningThe Lottery Supplies Independence Only

Randomization guarantees the independence condition of Lemma 5.1; it does not guarantee consistency. Consistency and the scalar notation \(Y_i(t)\) rest on SUTVA (Definition 4.2): no interference between units and no hidden versions of treatment. These are assumptions about the science, and randomization alone does not guarantee them; study design can help, for example by separating units or standardizing how treatment is delivered.

A second gap is compliance. Write \(Z_i\) for the randomized assignment and \(D_i\) for the treatment received. The design randomizes \(Z\), never \(D\). This chapter assumes full compliance, \(D_i = Z_i\), and writes \(T_i\) for their common value. When compliance fails, \(Z\) remains randomized and identifies the effect of assignment (the intention-to-treat effect), but the received treatment \(D\) need not retain the independence supplied by random assignment, and outcomes among those who received \(t\) no longer reflect the effect of receiving \(t\). This is the subject of Chapters 7 and 13.

NoteRemark: Randomization as Graph Surgery

In the language of Chapter 3, randomization deletes every arrow into \(T\) from the scientific system and replaces it with an arrow from an exogenous randomizer. In the resulting randomized-assignment graph, \(T\) is still random, but it shares no cause with \(Y\), so the back-door criterion holds with the empty adjustment set. This graph is not the intervention graph \(\Gcal_{\overline{T}}\): under \(\doop(T = t)\) the treatment is fixed at one value, whereas here it is random. What the two graphs share is the absence of substantive arrows into \(T\). Let \(G^R\) denote the randomized-assignment graph. In \(G^R_{\underline{T}}\) the outgoing arrows from \(T\) are removed. The arrow from the randomizer \(R\) into \(T\) remains, but \(R\) has no other connection to \(Y\), so \(Y\) and \(T\) are d-separated in \(G^R_{\underline{T}}\). Rule 2 of the do-calculus, applied within the randomized law \(p_R\), therefore gives \(p_R(y \mid T = t) = p_R\{y \mid \doop(T = t)\}\). Randomization changes only the treatment-assignment mechanism, so this interventional law equals the original system’s law under \(\doop(T = t)\): \[p_R(y \mid T = t) \;=\; f\bigl(y \mid \doop(T = t)\bigr). \tag{5.2}\] The first step applies Rule 2 within one regime; the second uses the invariance of the outcome mechanism across the two regimes. Problem 1 develops this argument.

5.1.4 Neyman’s Theorem: Exact Unbiasedness and Design Variance

The four-unit example computed the mean and variance of the randomization distribution by listing all six assignments. Neyman’s theorem gives both in closed form for any \(N\) and \(N_1\) (Neyman 1923). Define the finite-population variances and covariance (divisor \(N - 1\)) \[\begin{aligned} S_t^2 &= \frac1{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\bigl\{Y_i(t) - \bar Y(t)\bigr\}^2, \qquad t \in \{0, 1\},\\ S_{01} &= \frac1{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\bigl\{Y_i(0) - \bar Y(0)\bigr\}\bigl\{Y_i(1) - \bar Y(1)\bigr\}, \end{aligned}\] and the finite-population variance of the unit-level effects, \(S_\tau^2 = (N-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N(\tau_i - \bar\tau_N)^2 = S_1^2 + S_0^2 - 2S_{01}\).

Theorem 5.1 (Neyman’s Theorem for the Difference in Means) Under the CRD, with expectations and variances taken over the randomization distribution of \(\mathbf T\) given \(\mathcal F_N\):

  1. Unbiasedness. \(\E\bigl(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_N\bigr) = \bar\tau_N\).
  2. Design variance. \[\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_N\bigr) \;=\; \frac{S_1^2}{N_1} + \frac{S_0^2}{N_0} - \frac{S_\tau^2}{N} \;=\; \Bigl(\frac1{N_1} - \frac1N\Bigr)S_1^2 + \Bigl(\frac1{N_0} - \frac1N\Bigr)S_0^2 + \frac2N\,S_{01}. \tag{5.3}\]
  3. Conservative variance estimator. Let \(N_1, N_0 \ge 2\) and let \(\hat S_t^2\) be the sample variance of the observed outcomes in arm \(t\). Then \[\hat V \;=\; \frac{\hat S_1^2}{N_1} + \frac{\hat S_0^2}{N_0} \tag{5.4}\] satisfies \(\E(\hat V \mid \mathcal F_N) - \mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_N) = S_\tau^2/N \ge 0\). It is unbiased if and only if the unit-level effects are constant.
NoteProof (second reading)

Work conditionally on \(\mathcal F_N\), and let \(f_1 = N_1/N\). Under the CRD, \(\E(T_i) = f_1\) for every \(i\), and \[\mathrm{Cov}(T_i, T_j) = \begin{cases} f_1(1 - f_1), & i = j,\\[2pt] -\dfrac{f_1(1 - f_1)}{N - 1}, & i \ne j, \end{cases} \tag{5.5}\] because \(\E(T_iT_j) = N_1(N_1 - 1)/\{N(N - 1)\}\) for \(i \ne j\) when \(N_1\) units are drawn without replacement. By consistency, \(\bar Y_1 = N_1^{-1}\sum_i T_iY_i(1)\) and \(\bar Y_0 = N_0^{-1}\sum_i (1 - T_i)Y_i(0)\).

(i) \(\E(\bar Y_1) = N_1^{-1}\sum_i f_1Y_i(1) = \bar Y(1)\), and likewise \(\E(\bar Y_0) = \bar Y(0)\).

(ii) Let \(u_i = Y_i(1) - \bar Y(1)\) and \(v_i = Y_i(0) - \bar Y(0)\), so that \(\sum_iu_i = \sum_iv_i = 0\). Subtracting a constant does not change variances, so by Equation 5.5, \[\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y_1) = \frac{f_1(1 - f_1)}{N_1^2}\Bigl\{\sum_iu_i^2 - \frac1{N-1}\sum_{i\ne j}u_iu_j\Bigr\} = \frac{f_1(1 - f_1)}{N_1^2}\cdot\frac{N}{N-1}\sum_iu_i^2 = \Bigl(\frac1{N_1} - \frac1N\Bigr)S_1^2,\] using \(\sum_{i\ne j}u_iu_j = -\sum_iu_i^2\). By symmetry, \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y_0) = (N_0^{-1} - N^{-1})S_0^2\). Since \(1 - T_j\) has \(\mathrm{Cov}(T_i, 1 - T_j) = -\mathrm{Cov}(T_i, T_j)\), the same collapsing identity gives \(\mathrm{Cov}(\bar Y_1, \bar Y_0) = -S_{01}/N\). Hence \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}) = (N_1^{-1} - N^{-1})S_1^2 + (N_0^{-1} - N^{-1})S_0^2 + 2S_{01}/N\), the second form of Equation 5.3. The first form follows from \(S_\tau^2 = S_1^2 + S_0^2 - 2S_{01}\).

(iii) The treated arm is a simple random sample of size \(N_1\) from the \(N\) units, so the sample variance is unbiased for the population variance: \(\E(\hat S_1^2) = S_1^2\), and likewise \(\E(\hat S_0^2) = S_0^2\). Hence \(\E(\hat V) = S_1^2/N_1 + S_0^2/N_0\), and subtracting the first form of Equation 5.3 gives \(S_\tau^2/N\). \(\square\)

Example 5.3 (Neyman’s Theorem in the Four-Unit Example) From Table 5.1, \(S_1^2 = 35/12\), \(S_0^2 = 59/12\), and \(S_\tau^2 = 1/3\). With \(N_1 = N_0 = 2\) and \(N = 4\), the design variance is \[\frac{35/12}{2} + \frac{59/12}{2} - \frac{1/3}{4} = \frac{35}{24} + \frac{59}{24} - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{23}{6},\] the variance of the six values found in Example 5.2. Averaging \(\hat V\) over the six assignments gives \(\E(\hat V \mid \mathcal F_4) = 47/12 \approx 3.92\), larger than \(23/6 \approx 3.83\) by \(1/12 = S_\tau^2/N\). The individual values of \(\hat V\), however, range from \(5/4\) (cases 1 and 6) to \(13/2\) (case 4). Conservative here means that \(\hat V\) overestimates the design variance in expectation; it need not exceed it for every realized assignment.

NoteRemark: Why the Conservatism Cannot Be Removed

The correction \(S_\tau^2/N\) depends on the variance of the unit-level effects. Computing it requires both \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) for every unit, which the fundamental problem forbids. The exact design variance is therefore not identified without restrictions on the joint distribution of the potential outcomes. \(\hat V\) is the conventional conservative choice, not the sharpest one: bounds on the unidentified covariance \(S_{01}\), implied by the two observed marginal distributions, give tighter conservative estimators. Under a constant effect the correction vanishes and \(\hat V\) is unbiased.

5.1.5 The Difference in Means as a Regression Slope

The difference in means is also an ordinary least squares (OLS) coefficient. Minimizing \(\sum_{i=1}^N(Y_i - \alpha - \tau T_i)^2\) gives \(\hat\alpha = \bar Y_0\) and \(\hat\tau = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0 = \hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\). The regression view connects this section to the covariate-adjusted estimators of Section 5.3 and Section 5.4, but its standard errors need care.

A heteroskedastic regression. In a superpopulation reading, let \(Y_i(t) = \mu_t + e_i(t)\) with \(\E\{e_i(t)\} = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}\{e_i(t)\} = \sigma_t^2\); the two errors of a unit may be correlated. Then \[Y_i = \mu_0 + \tau T_i + e_i, \qquad e_i = T_ie_i(1) + (1 - T_i)e_i(0), \qquad \tau = \mu_1 - \mu_0,\] and randomization gives \(\E(e_i \mid T_i) = 0\). But \(\mathrm{Var}(e_i \mid T_i = t) = \sigma_t^2\): the error variance differs between arms. The classical OLS standard error pools the two variances. With \(N_1, N_0 \ge 2\), \[\hat V_{\mathrm{pooled}} - \hat V = \frac{(N - 1)(N_1 - N_0)(\hat S_1^2 - \hat S_0^2)}{(N - 2)N_1N_0},\] so the pooled standard error agrees with Neyman’s estimator for every data set under balanced allocation. With unequal arm sizes the two generally differ, although they also agree when the arm-specific sample variances are equal.

Proposition 5.1 (The Leverage-Corrected Sandwich Variance Equals Neyman’s Estimator) In the regression of \(Y_i\) on \((1, T_i)\), let \(\hat e_i\) be the OLS residuals and \(h_{ii}\) the leverages. The heteroskedasticity-robust (sandwich) variance estimator of \(\hat\tau\) in which each squared residual is divided by \(1 - h_{ii}\) (the “HC2” estimator) equals \(\hat V = \hat S_1^2/N_1 + \hat S_0^2/N_0\), for any \(N_1, N_0 \ge 2\).

Proof. \(\hat\tau = \sum_ia_iY_i\) with \(a_i = 1/N_1\) for treated units and \(a_i = -1/N_0\) for controls, and the robust variance with this residual correction is \(\sum_ia_i^2\hat e_i^2/(1 - h_{ii})\). The fitted value of unit \(i\) is its arm mean, so \(\hat e_i = Y_i - \bar Y_t\) for \(i\) in arm \(t\), and \(h_{ii} = 1/N_t\). For the treated arm, \[\sum_{i:T_i=1}\frac{\hat e_i^2}{N_1^2(1 - 1/N_1)} = \frac{1}{N_1}\cdot\frac{1}{N_1 - 1}\sum_{i:T_i=1}(Y_i - \bar Y_1)^2 = \frac{\hat S_1^2}{N_1},\] and likewise for the control arm. \(\square\)

Section 5.4 adds covariates to this regression. Proposition 5.1 is the simplest instance of a general fact used there: a sandwich variance from the regression estimates the design variance conservatively.

5.1.6 Finite-Population and Superpopulation Targets

Neyman’s theorem conditions on the \(N\) enrolled units and targets \(\bar\tau_N\). Much of the literature instead targets the superpopulation ATE \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E\{Y(1) - Y(0)\}\). Table 5.3 contrasts the two frameworks.

Randomization alone justifies design-based inference for \(\bar\tau_N\), the effect among the units randomized (internal validity). Extending the conclusion to a wider population requires random sampling or a transportability argument (external validity). The next remark shows how the two variances relate when the units are themselves a random sample.

NoteRemark: Superpopulation Variance as a Two-Phase Corollary

Suppose the units are i.i.d. draws from a superpopulation in which \((Y_i(1), Y_i(0))\) has variances \((\sigma_1^2, \sigma_0^2)\) and covariance \(\sigma_{01}\). Phase one draws \(\mathcal F_N\); phase two assigns treatment by the CRD. By the law of total variance and Theorem 5.1 (i), \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}) = \mathrm{Var}(\bar\tau_N) + \E\bigl\{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_N)\bigr\}.\] Under i.i.d. sampling, \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar\tau_N) = (\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2 - 2\sigma_{01})/N\), and taking expectations in the second form of Equation 5.3, with \(\E(S_t^2) = \sigma_t^2\) and \(\E(S_{01}) = \sigma_{01}\), gives \((N_1^{-1} - N^{-1})\sigma_1^2 + (N_0^{-1} - N^{-1})\sigma_0^2 + 2\sigma_{01}/N\). Adding, \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{N_1} + \frac{\sigma_0^2}{N_0}. \tag{5.6}\] The Neyman correction \(S_\tau^2/N\) is exactly cancelled by the phase-one variance of \(\bar\tau_N\). Equation Equation 5.6 is the formula usually reported in textbooks; Theorem 5.1 is its finite-population refinement. See Ding (2024).

The parallel with survey sampling. A completely randomized experiment is simple random sampling without replacement from the finite population of potential outcomes: the treated arm is a sample of the \(Y_i(1)\), and the control arm, its complement, is a sample of the \(Y_i(0)\). This is why Neyman’s variance formula has the form of a survey-sampling variance. Table 5.4 sets out the parallel; it returns in Section 5.4, where the regression estimator of survey sampling becomes a covariate-adjusted estimator of \(\bar\tau_N\).

NoteRemark: Randomization Justifies Inference without a Population Model

In the design-based framework the potential outcomes are fixed numbers attached to the \(N\) units, and the only randomness is the assignment vector, drawn from a finite, enumerable set. The i.i.d. assumption that underlies most model-based procedures is not needed: the units may be a convenience sample, a census, or a small targeted group. The contrast with observational studies concerns the treatment-assignment mechanism, not randomness in general. Two caveats keep the comparison honest: randomizing treatment does not make a convenience sample representative of any wider population, and observational data collected by probability sampling still face the identification problem, even though their sampling inference is design-based.

5.1.7 Fisher’s Randomization Test

Neyman’s theorem uses the randomization to estimate an average effect. Fisher used it to test a hypothesis exactly (Fisher 1935). The hypothesis is the sharp null of no effect for any unit, \[H_0:\; Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) \quad\text{for every } i = 1, \ldots, N.\] Under \(H_0\) each unit’s outcome does not depend on its assignment, so the observed outcome vector \(\mathbf Y\) would have been the same under every assignment. The whole science table is then known, and the randomization distribution of any test statistic can be computed exactly. The two enumerations of this section differ in this respect: Table 5.2 uses the given potential-outcome schedule, so the observed outcomes change with the assignment, while the Fisher enumeration uses the schedule implied by the sharp null, so the outcomes stay fixed.

Example 5.4 (Fisher’s Test in the Four-Unit Example) Suppose case 3 of Table 5.2 happened: units 1 and 4 were treated, and \(\mathbf Y = (4, 3, 5, 8)\), so \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} = 6 - 4 = 2\). Under \(H_0\) the vector \(\mathbf Y\) stays the same under every assignment. Recomputing the difference in means for the six assignments, holding \(\mathbf Y\) fixed, gives \[\text{treated }\{1,2\}: -3,\quad \{1,3\}: -1,\quad \{1,4\}: 2,\quad \{2,3\}: -2,\quad \{2,4\}: 1,\quad \{3,4\}: 3 .\] Two of the six values are at least 2, so the one-sided \(p\)-value is \(2/6 = 1/3\); four have absolute value at least 2, so the two-sided \(p\)-value is \(4/6\). With \(N = 4\) the smallest attainable one-sided \(p\)-value is \(1/6\). For the two-sided test based on \(|\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}|\) it is \(2/6\), because with balanced allocation complementary assignments give the same absolute value. Either way, this experiment can never reject at the 5% level, however large the effect. The true effects in Table 5.1 are 2 or 3, so \(H_0\) is false; the test simply has no power at \(N = 4\).

Proposition 5.2 (Fisher Randomization Test) Let \(Q = Q(\mathbf T, \mathbf Y)\) be a test statistic, for example \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\), and let \(\mathbf T^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf T^{(M)}\) be all assignments the design allows. Assume that each has the same probability \(1/M\), as under the CRD with \(M = \binom{N}{N_1}\). Define \[p_{\mathrm{FRT}} = \frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M \mathbb{I}\bigl\{Q(\mathbf T^{(k)}, \mathbf Y) \ge Q(\mathbf T, \mathbf Y)\bigr\}.\] Under \(H_0\), \(P(p_{\mathrm{FRT}} \le u) \le u\) for every \(u \in (0, 1)\), in finite samples.

Proof. Under \(H_0\), \(\mathbf Y\) is fixed, so the \(M\) values \(q_k = Q(\mathbf T^{(k)}, \mathbf Y)\) are fixed numbers, and the observed statistic is \(q_K\) for an index \(K\) that is uniform on \(\{1, \ldots, M\}\). Write \(p_k = M^{-1}\#\{j : q_j \ge q_k\}\), so \(p_{\mathrm{FRT}} = p_K\). Since \(p_k\) is nonincreasing in \(q_k\), the set \(A = \{k : p_k \le u\}\) has the form \(\{k : q_k \ge c\}\) for some \(c\). If \(A\) is nonempty, let \(k^\ast \in A\) have the smallest \(q_k\) in \(A\); every member of \(A\) has \(q_k \ge q_{k^\ast}\), so \(|A|/M \le p_{k^\ast} \le u\). Hence \(P(p_{\mathrm{FRT}} \le u) = P(K \in A) = |A|/M \le u\). \(\square\)

For a design with unequal assignment probabilities, the exhaustive tail probability must weight each assignment by its actual probability. Three practical points follow. First, \(M\) grows quickly with \(N\). In practice one draws \(B\) assignments \(\mathbf T^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf T^{(B)}\) independently from the actual assignment mechanism, independently of the observed assignment. The fraction of sampled statistics at least as large as the observed one estimates \(p_{\mathrm{FRT}}\), but it is not itself a valid test \(p\)-value for finite \(B\); it can even be zero. A valid, possibly conservative, Monte Carlo \(p\)-value is \[p_{\mathrm{MC}} = \frac{1 + \sum_{b=1}^B \mathbb{I}\bigl\{Q(\mathbf T^{(b)}, \mathbf Y) \ge Q(\mathbf T, \mathbf Y)\bigr\}}{B + 1}:\] under the sharp null the observed statistic and the \(B\) simulated ones are exchangeable, so the rank of the observed statistic among all \(B + 1\) values gives the test (Phipson and Smyth 2010). Second, for a two-sided test use a two-sided statistic, such as \(|\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}|\). Third, the reference set must match the actual design: a permutation scheme that does not follow the assignment mechanism is not a Fisher randomization test. Fisher’s exact test for a \(2 \times 2\) table is the special case with a binary outcome.

NoteRemark: Unidentifiable Effects, Exactly Testable Hypothesis

The unit-level effects \(\tau_i\) are not identified, yet the sharp null is exactly testable. The two facts are consistent. A value of a single \(\tau_i\) is a statement about one unobserved potential outcome. The sharp null is a joint restriction on all \(N\) units that, when true, fills in every missing potential outcome. By contrast, the weak null \(\bar\tau_N = 0\) does not fill in the missing outcomes, so it does not generate an exact randomization distribution; testing it is Neyman’s territory.

Fisher and Neyman ask different questions. Both frameworks rest on the known assignment mechanism, but they answer different questions (Table 5.5). The two are complementary rather than contradictory.

NoteRemark: Further Reading on the Randomization Approach

Ding (2024) gives a graduate-level treatment of the randomization framework, including stratified experiments and regression adjustment. The finite-population central limit theorem behind large-sample confidence intervals for \(\bar\tau_N\), which needs neither i.i.d. observations nor a superpopulation model, is developed by Li and Ding (2017). For inference about the heterogeneity of effects across units, Ding et al. (2016) develop randomization-based tests and confidence intervals that apply directly to completely randomized experiments.

5.2 Ignorability: A Short Review

Section 5.1 showed that randomization makes the potential outcomes independent of the assignment, so the difference in means targets the ATE without any model. In an observational study the treatment is not assigned by the researcher; it is selected by patients, firms, governments, or individuals, often on the basis of characteristics that also affect the outcome. Chapter 4 developed the assumption that replaces randomization in this setting. This section collects the parts of that development that the rest of the chapter uses.

Statistical adjustment can implement ignorability once it is assumed, but it cannot create it. No covariate adjustment, however flexible, closes a back-door path that no observed pretreatment variable intercepts.

5.2.1 Terminology and What Ignorability Requires

Strong ignorability (Definition 4.5; Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983)) combines joint unconfoundedness and overlap: \[\bigl\{Y(0), Y(1)\bigr\} \indep T \mid X \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0 < P(T = 1 \mid X) < 1 \ \text{ almost surely}.\] The weak (pointwise) form, \(Y(t) \indep T \mid X\) for each \(t\) separately, is implied by the joint form and is all that the identification of \(\E\{Y(t)\}\) uses. Neither form identifies the joint law of \(\{Y(0), Y(1)\}\), for example \(P\{Y(1) > Y(0)\}\); that requires cross-world restrictions (Chapter 4). Other names for the same idea are unconfoundedness, selection on observables, no unmeasured confounders, and conditional exchangeability. Authors differ on weak versus joint forms and on whether positivity and consistency are included.

Ignorability is a substantive assumption, not a statistical one. A sufficient graphical condition is an observed pretreatment set \(X\) that blocks every back-door path from \(T\) to \(Y\). This does not require observing every common cause: in the graph \(T \leftarrow U \to W \to Y\) of Chapter 3, the unobserved \(U\) is harmless because the observed \(W\) blocks the only back-door path. The phrase “no unmeasured confounders” should be read in this path-blocking sense.

WarningIgnorability Is Untestable from Observational Data

From the observed law of \((Y, T, X)\) alone, without additional structure, conditional exchangeability cannot be tested. If an unobserved \(U\) affects both \(T\) and \(Y\) directly, the only intermediate vertex on the path \(T \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y\) is \(U\) itself, so no observed adjustment set blocks it. Negative controls, proxies, or multiple outcomes can give falsifiable implications, but only under their own assumptions. Sensitivity analysis (Rosenbaum 2002) asks how strong an unobserved confounder must be to change a conclusion; it cannot confirm the assumption.

5.2.2 From Ignorability to Identification

Write \(\mu(t, x) = \E(Y \mid T = t, X = x)\) for the outcome regression.

Theorem 5.2 (Identification of the ATE and the ATT) Assume consistency, weak ignorability \(Y(t) \indep T \mid X\) for \(t \in \{0, 1\}\), overlap, and \(\E|Y(t)| < \infty\). Then \[\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E\{Y(1) - Y(0)\} = \int \bigl\{\mu(1, x) - \mu(0, x)\bigr\}\, p(x)\, dx, \tag{5.7}\] \[\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = \E\{Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 1\} = \int \bigl\{\mu(1, x) - \mu(0, x)\bigr\}\, p(x \mid T = 1)\, dx. \tag{5.8}\]

Proof. For the ATE, Proposition 4.2 gives \(\E\{Y(t)\} = \int \mu(t, x)\,p(x)\,dx\) for each \(t\); subtract. For the ATT, iterate expectations within the treated: \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = \E[\E\{Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 1, X\} \mid T = 1]\). Consistency gives \(\E\{Y(1) \mid T = 1, X\} = \mu(1, X)\). For the counterfactual mean, \(\E\{Y(0) \mid T = 1, X\} = \E\{Y(0) \mid X\} = \E\{Y(0) \mid T = 0, X\} = \mu(0, X)\), by ignorability (twice) and consistency; control-arm overlap on the support of \(X \mid T = 1\) makes the last conditional mean well defined. Integrate against \(p(x \mid T = 1)\). \(\square\)

NoteRemark: The ATT Needs Less than the ATE

The proof uses ignorability and overlap only for the control outcome: \(Y(0) \indep T \mid X\) and \(P(T = 0 \mid X) > 0\) on the support of the treated covariates. The ATT is therefore identified under weaker conditions than the ATE.

5.2.3 Which Variables to Condition On: The Pretreatment Requirement

The back-door formula conditions on \(X\). Before estimating anything, there is a prior question that is purely graphical: which variables belong in \(X\)? The answer is not “all available variables.”

WarningDo Not Condition on Post-Treatment Variables when Targeting the Total Effect

A variable \(L\) is post-treatment if it is a descendant of \(T\). Including \(L\) in \(X\) can bias the estimated effect, even when \(L\) predicts \(Y\) strongly. There are two mechanisms.

  1. Blocking a causal pathway. If \(T \to L \to Y\), conditioning on the mediator \(L\) blocks part of the effect and changes the target. It does not by itself identify a direct effect; that needs the estimands and assumptions of Chapter 8.
  2. Opening a collider path. If \(T \to L \leftarrow U \to Y\), the path is blocked until we condition on the collider \(L\). Conditioning opens it and can induce a spurious \(T\)–\(Y\) association.

Both failures violate the back-door condition that \(X\) contain no descendant of \(T\).

The practical rule. Classify every candidate covariate as pre-treatment or post-treatment using the causal graph, before running any regression. For the back-door adjustment strategy used here, choose a set of pretreatment covariates that satisfies the criterion; validity is a property of the set jointly, not of each variable separately.

NoteRemark: Choosing among Valid Adjustment Sets

The back-door criterion usually admits several valid sets, and all of them identify the same quantity. A superset of a valid set is not automatically valid: an added variable can open a collider path. Among valid sets, smaller sets reduce positivity and modeling burdens, but they are not uniformly more efficient: a pretreatment variable that predicts the outcome strongly can improve precision, while one that mainly predicts treatment can inflate variance. Graphical sufficiency settles validity; the choice among valid sets is an efficiency question. Section 5.4 gives a first instance.

5.3 Estimating the ATE by Outcome Regression

Identification is now settled: under the conditions of Theorem 5.2, \[\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E\bigl\{\mu(1, X) - \mu(0, X)\bigr\} = \int \bigl\{\mu(1, x) - \mu(0, x)\bigr\}\, p(x)\, dx .\] The right side contains only observable quantities, but it is a population quantity. This section turns it into an estimator and studies its sampling properties. Throughout, the data are \(N\) i.i.d. observations \((Y_i, T_i, X_i)\) from a superpopulation, \(X\) is a set of pretreatment covariates satisfying the back-door criterion, and we write \[P_N f = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(Y_i, T_i, X_i), \qquad P f = \E\{f(Y, T, X)\}.\] The letter \(N\) is the number of observed units here as in Section 5.1, but the probability model is different: there the units were fixed and the assignment random; here the units are a random sample.

5.3.1 The Plug-In Principle

The identified functional has three unknowns, and each has a natural sample replacement (Table 5.6).

The plug-in estimator is the difference of two regression estimators, \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}}, \qquad \hat\mu_{t,\mathrm{reg}} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \hat\mu(t, X_i), \tag{5.9}\] where \(\hat\mu_{t,\mathrm{reg}}\) is the regression estimator of \(\E\{Y(t)\}\). The averaging step needs no model for the covariate distribution; all assumptions of this section concern \(\hat\mu\).

5.3.2 The Discrete Case: Simpson’s Paradox and Standardization

When \(X\) is categorical, \(\mu(t, x)\) can be estimated by the cell mean \(\bar Y_{t,x}\) of \(Y\) among units with \(T = t\) and \(X = x\), and Equation 5.9 becomes the standardization estimator \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{STD}} = \sum_{x \in \mathcal X}\bigl(\bar Y_{1,x} - \bar Y_{0,x}\bigr)\,\hat p(x), \qquad \hat p(x) = \frac{N_x}{N}. \tag{5.10}\] This is \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) with a saturated outcome model, one free parameter per cell, so no functional-form assumption is made. It is also called the g-formula (Robins 1986) and direct standardization.

Example 5.5 (Simpson’s Paradox as a Standardization) Return to the vaccination data of Chapter 1 (Section 1.4.3): \(N = 1{,}000\) people, with \(X = 1\) for the elderly. The vaccinated group contains 360 elderly and 60 young people; the unvaccinated group, 40 elderly and 540 young. The risks of illness are \(\mu(1, \text{elderly}) = 30\%\), \(\mu(0, \text{elderly}) = 50\%\), \(\mu(1, \text{young}) = 5\%\), and \(\mu(0, \text{young}) = 10\%\), so the vaccine lowers the risk in both strata. Three contrasts weight the strata differently.

  • Pooled. Each arm uses its own covariate mix, \(P(X = 1 \mid T = 1) = 360/420 = 6/7\) and \(P(X = 1 \mid T = 0) = 40/580 \approx 0.069\): \[100\Bigl(\frac{111}{420} - \frac{74}{580}\Bigr) \approx 26.43\% - 12.76\% \approx +13.7 \text{ percentage points}.\]
  • ATE (standardized). Both arms use \(P(X = 1) = 0.40\): \(0.4(-20) + 0.6(-5) = -11.0\) percentage points.
  • ATT. Both arms use \(P(X \mid T = 1)\): \(\tfrac67(-20) + \tfrac17(-5) \approx -17.9\) percentage points.

The pooled comparison reverses the sign because the vaccinated are mostly elderly, who have a higher baseline risk. The ATT is more negative than the ATE because the treated are concentrated in the stratum with the larger effect. The reversal itself is a fact about conditional proportions; which comparison is causal is a fact about the graph. The standardized answers are causal only under the maintained assumptions: age is a sufficient adjustment set, with positivity and consistency.

5.3.3 The Outcome Model and the Regression Estimator

When \(X\) is continuous or has many categories, cell means are not available, and \(\mu\) is modeled.

NoteThe Three-Step Recipe (Outcome Regression, g-Computation)

Assume \(\mu(t, x) = m(t, x; \beta)\) with \(m\) known and \(\beta\) finite-dimensional.

  1. Fit. Estimate \(\beta\) by least squares (or maximum likelihood) and set \(\hat\mu(t, x) = m(t, x; \hat\beta)\).
  2. Predict. For every unit, whatever its observed treatment, compute \(\hat\mu(1, X_i)\) and \(\hat\mu(0, X_i)\). These are predicted conditional means \(\E\{Y(t) \mid X = X_i\}\), not the unit’s own potential outcomes, and their difference is not an estimate of the unit-level effect.
  3. Average. \(\hat\mu_{t,\mathrm{reg}} = N^{-1}\sum_i\hat\mu(t, X_i)\) and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}}\), as in Equation 5.9.

For the ATT, average the fitted contrasts over the treated units only: \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = N_1^{-1}\sum_{i:\,T_i=1}\{\hat\mu(1, X_i) - \hat\mu(0, X_i)\}\). The variant \(N_1^{-1}\sum_{i:\,T_i=1}\{Y_i - \hat\mu(0, X_i)\}\) uses the observed treated outcomes and models \(\mu(0, \cdot)\) only, matching the preceding remark on the ATT.

Two linear choices of \(m\) are common.

  • Model (a), additive: \(m(t, x; \beta) = \alpha + \tau t + \gamma^\top x\). Then \(m(1, x) - m(0, x) = \tau\) for every \(x\), and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is the OLS coefficient on \(T\). The model imposes the same conditional average effect at every covariate value, so it forces \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \tau_{\mathrm{ATT}}\); it does not require identical unit-level effects.
  • Model (b), one model per arm: \(m(t, x; \beta) = \alpha_t + \gamma_t^\top x\), fitted separately on each arm. Then \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = (\hat\alpha_1 - \hat\alpha_0) + (\hat\gamma_1 - \hat\gamma_0)^\top \bar X\), with \(\bar X = N^{-1}\sum_iX_i\).

Proposition 5.3 (Model (b): Each Regression Estimator Is an Intercept after Centering) Under model (b), write \(\hat\mu(t, x) = \hat a_t + \hat\gamma_t^\top(x - \bar X)\) with \(\hat a_t = \hat\alpha_t + \hat\gamma_t^\top\bar X\). Then

  1. \(\hat\mu_{t,\mathrm{reg}} = \hat a_t\), the intercept of the arm-\(t\) regression of \(Y\) on \((1, X - \bar X)\), so \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat a_1 - \hat a_0\);
  2. in the single regression of \(Y\) on \(\bigl(1,\, T,\, X - \bar X,\, T(X - \bar X)\bigr)\) fitted to all \(N\) units, the coefficient on \(T\) equals \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\).

Proof. (i) Averaging over all \(N\) units, \(N^{-1}\sum_i\hat\mu(t, X_i) = \hat a_t + \hat\gamma_t^\top(\bar X - \bar X) = \hat a_t\). (ii) The single regression has a separate intercept and slope for each arm, so its fitted values on arm \(t\) are those of the arm-\(t\) regression on \((1, X - \bar X)\). Its intercept is \(\hat a_0\), and the coefficient on \(T\) is \(\hat a_1 - \hat a_0\). \(\square\)

Centering at the arm-specific mean \(\bar X_t\) instead of the full-sample mean \(\bar X\) would make the intercept equal \(\bar Y_t\), and no adjustment would take place. Under model (b) the ATT estimator is \(\hat a_1 - \hat a_0 + (\hat\gamma_1 - \hat\gamma_0)^\top(\bar X_1 - \bar X)\), with \(\bar X_1\) the treated-arm covariate mean.

With the cell means as \(\hat\mu\), the within-stratum contrasts are \(42 - 35 = 7\) for \(X = 0\) and \(58 - 50 = 8\) for \(X = 1\). In the full sample, half the units have \(X = 1\), so \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = 0.5(7) + 0.5(8) = 7.5, \qquad \hat\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = 0.3(7) + 0.7(8) = 7.7 .\] The adjusted estimates are roughly half the unadjusted difference. 5.3.5 The Goal, the Obstacle, and the Nuisance Parameter

The goal is statistical inference with \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\): consistency for \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\), an asymptotic distribution, and a variance estimate, so that a confidence interval can be reported. The obstacle is that \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is not a sample mean. It averages fitted values, and the data enter through \(\hat\beta\) inside \(m\), so the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem do not apply to it directly.

The source of the difficulty is a second parameter. The parameter of interest is \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E\{\mu(1, X) - \mu(0, X)\}\). The nuisance parameter is \(\beta\), the coefficient of the outcome model, and more generally the regression function \(\mu\) itself. It is not wanted for its own sake, but \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\) depends on it, so it must be estimated first, and its estimation error propagates into \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\). The same structure returns in later chapters, with the propensity score (Chapter 6) and with two nuisance functions (Chapters 11 and 12).

For a linear outcome model the propagation can be written exactly. Let \[\mu(t, x) = z(t, x)^\top\beta_0, \qquad z_d(x) = z(1, x) - z(0, x), \qquad Z_i = z(T_i, X_i),\] with \(z\) known, and let \(\hat\beta = (P_NZZ^\top)^{-1}P_NZY\) be the OLS estimator. For model (b), \(z(t, x) = (t,\, 1 - t,\, t\,x^\top,\, (1 - t)\,x^\top)^\top\), \(\beta = (\alpha_1, \alpha_0, \gamma_1^\top, \gamma_0^\top)^\top\), and \(z_d(x) = (1, -1, x^\top, -x^\top)^\top\). For model (a), \(z(t, x) = (1, t, x^\top)^\top\) and \(z_d = (0, 1, 0^\top)^\top\) is constant. Because \(m\) is linear in \(\beta\), the average of the predictions is the prediction at the average: \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = P_N\bigl(z_d^\top\hat\beta\bigr) = (P_Nz_d)^\top\hat\beta, \qquad \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = (Pz_d)^\top\beta_0 , \tag{5.11}\] where the second equality uses Theorem 5.2 and the correct specification of the model. For model (b), \(P_Nz_d = (1, -1, \bar X^\top, -\bar X^\top)^\top\). The nuisance enters \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) through one vector, \(\hat\beta\).

5.3.6 Consistency: Estimation Error and Sampling Error

Subtracting the two displays in Equation 5.11 gives an exact decomposition: \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \underbrace{(P_Nz_d)^\top(\hat\beta - \beta_0)}_{\text{estimation error}} + \underbrace{(P_N - P)\bigl(z_d^\top\beta_0\bigr)}_{\text{sampling error}} . \tag{5.12}\] The estimation error comes from estimating the nuisance parameter; the sampling error comes from averaging over the sampled covariates instead of their population distribution.

Proposition 5.4 (Consistency of the Regression Estimator) Assume the conditions of Theorem 5.2, i.i.d. data, a correctly specified linear model \(\E(Y \mid T, X) = Z^\top\beta_0\), finite second moments of \(Y\) and \(Z\), \(\E\|z_d(X)\| < \infty\), and a nonsingular \(A = \E(ZZ^\top)\). Then \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} \pto \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\).

Proof. OLS is a continuous function of the averages \(P_NZZ^\top\) and \(P_NZY\), which converge to \(A\) and \(\E(ZY) = A\beta_0\) by the law of large numbers and correct specification; hence \(\hat\beta \pto \beta_0\). Since \(P_Nz_d \pto Pz_d\), the estimation error tends to zero by Slutsky’s theorem. The sampling error is the average of i.i.d. mean-zero terms, so it tends to zero by the law of large numbers. \(\square\)

The two assumptions have two different jobs. Identification (Theorem 5.2) makes \(\E\{\mu(1, X) - \mu(0, X)\}\) the ATE. The outcome model makes \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) consistent for it, because correct specification is what makes OLS converge to the \(\beta_0\) that defines \(\mu\). Under model (a), \(z_d\) is constant, so the sampling error vanishes and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\tau_{\mathrm{OLS}}\).

5.3.7 Asymptotic Normality and Variance Estimation

The decomposition Equation 5.12 reduces the problem to the asymptotics of the nuisance estimator.

Lemma 5.2 (OLS under a Correct Linear Model) Let \(Y = Z^\top\beta_0 + \varepsilon\) with \(\E(\varepsilon \mid Z) = 0\), i.i.d. data, \(\E\|Z\|^4 < \infty\), \(\E\varepsilon^4 < \infty\), and \(A = \E(ZZ^\top)\) nonsingular; \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon \mid Z)\) may depend on \(Z\). With \(B = \E(\varepsilon^2ZZ^\top)\), \[\sqrt N(\hat\beta - \beta_0) = A^{-1}\sqrt N\,P_N(Z\varepsilon) + o_p(1) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N\bigl(0,\, A^{-1}BA^{-1}\bigr).\]

Proof. Substituting \(Y = Z^\top\beta_0 + \varepsilon\) into \(\hat\beta\) gives the exact identity \(\hat\beta - \beta_0 = (P_NZZ^\top)^{-1}P_N(Z\varepsilon)\). By the law of large numbers, \((P_NZZ^\top)^{-1} \pto A^{-1}\). The terms \(Z_i\varepsilon_i\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\E\{Z\,\E(\varepsilon \mid Z)\} = 0\) and variance \(B\), so \(\sqrt N P_N(Z\varepsilon) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, B)\) by the central limit theorem; Slutsky’s theorem completes the proof. \(\square\)

The matrix \(A^{-1}BA^{-1}\) is the sandwich variance. It is estimated by \(\hat A^{-1}\hat B\hat A^{-1}\) with \(\hat A = P_NZZ^\top\), \(\hat B = P_N\hat\varepsilon^2ZZ^\top\), and \(\hat\varepsilon_i = Y_i - Z_i^\top\hat\beta\). Under homoskedasticity, \(B = \sigma^2A\) and it reduces to the classical \(\sigma^2A^{-1}\).

Theorem 5.3 (Asymptotic Normality of the Regression Estimator) Under the conditions of Proposition 5.4 and Lemma 5.2, assume also \(\E\|z_d(X)\|^2 < \infty\), and let \(c = Pz_d\) and \[\varphi_i = \bigl\{z_d(X_i)^\top\beta_0 - \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\bigr\} + c^\top A^{-1}Z_i\varepsilon_i . \tag{5.13}\] Then \(\sqrt N(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}) = \sqrt N\,P_N\varphi + o_p(1) \xrightarrow{d} N\{0, \mathrm{Var}(\varphi)\}\), and \[\mathrm{Var}(\varphi) = \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\bigl\{z_d(X)^\top\beta_0\bigr\}}_{\text{sampling error}} + \underbrace{c^\top A^{-1}BA^{-1}c}_{\text{estimation error}} . \tag{5.14}\]

Proof. Multiply Equation 5.12 by \(\sqrt N\). By Lemma 5.2, \(\sqrt N(\hat\beta - \beta_0) = O_p(1)\), and \(P_Nz_d = c + o_p(1)\), so the estimation error is \(c^\top A^{-1}\sqrt N P_N(Z\varepsilon) + o_p(1)\). Adding the sampling error gives \(\sqrt N P_N\varphi + o_p(1)\), and the central limit theorem applies to the i.i.d. mean-zero terms \(\varphi_i\), which have finite variance by the moment conditions. For the variance, the correct conditional-mean model gives \(\E(\varepsilon \mid T, X) = 0\), and both \(z_d(X)\) and \(Z\) are functions of \((T, X)\), so the cross term is \[\E\bigl[\{z_d(X)^\top\beta_0 - \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\}\,c^\top A^{-1}Z\, \E(\varepsilon \mid T, X)\bigr] = 0 . \qquad \square\]

The moment condition on \(z_d(X)\) is not implied by those on \(Z\) for a general \(z(t, x)\): \(Z\) involves only the received treatment, and a component that matters only under the unreceived treatment can be large where that treatment is rare. For models (a) and (b), \(z_d(X)\) is a subvector of \(Z\) up to sign, or constant, and the condition is automatic.

The function \(\varphi\) is the influence function of \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\): to first order, the estimator behaves like the sample mean of \(\varphi_i\). Its two components are the two errors of Equation 5.12, to first order, and they are uncorrelated because the correct model gives \(\E(\varepsilon \mid T, X) = 0\). The exact finite-sample terms of Equation 5.12 need not be uncorrelated.

Variance estimation. With \(\hat c = P_Nz_d\) and the plug-in quantities above, \[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}) = \frac1N\Bigl[P_N\bigl\{z_d(X)^\top\hat\beta - \hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\bigr\}^2 + \hat c^\top\hat A^{-1}\hat B\hat A^{-1}\hat c\Bigr] \tag{5.15}\] is consistent for \(N^{-1}\mathrm{Var}(\varphi)\) by the law of large numbers and continuity, and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\) is an asymptotic 95% confidence interval. An asymptotically equivalent estimator is \(N^{-2}\sum_i\hat\varphi_i^2\), with \(\hat\varphi_i\) the plug-in influence values. The two are not identical in finite samples: the normal equations do not make the sample cross term between the two components vanish, but it is \(o_p(N^{-1})\).

WarningTwo Shortcuts That Miss a Component

The sample variance of the fitted contrasts alone omits the estimation error; under model (a) it is zero. Under model (b), even the sandwich standard error of the \(T\) coefficient in the centered interacted regression of Proposition 5.3 omits the sampling error from averaging over a random covariate distribution. Under model (a) the sampling error is zero, and Equation 5.15 reduces to the sandwich variance of the \(T\) coefficient; the classical OLS standard error is valid under homoskedasticity, and the sandwich also allows heteroskedasticity.

NoteRemark: The Sandwich Formula for a General M-Estimator

OLS is one M-estimator. If \(\hat\beta\) solves \(P_N\psi(O; \beta) = 0\) and \(\beta_0\) solves \(P\psi(O; \beta) = 0\), then under regularity conditions \(\sqrt N(\hat\beta - \beta_0) = -\dot\Psi^{-1}\sqrt N P_N\psi(O; \beta_0) + o_p(1)\), with \(\dot\Psi = \E\{\partial\psi(O; \beta_0)/\partial\beta^\top\}\), and the asymptotic variance is \(\dot\Psi^{-1}\E(\psi\psi^\top)\dot\Psi^{-\top}\). Least squares has \(\psi = Z(Y - Z^\top\beta)\), \(\dot\Psi = -A\), and \(\E(\psi\psi^\top) = B\). The extension of Theorem 5.3 to an outcome model \(m(t, x; \beta)\) fitted by an M-estimator, for example logistic regression, involves two derivatives. Let \(\delta(X; \beta) = m(1, X; \beta) - m(0, X; \beta)\) and \(c = \E\{\partial_\beta\delta(X; \beta_0)\}\). If \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E\{\delta(X; \beta_0)\}\) and the usual differentiability and moment conditions hold, the influence function of \(P_N\delta(X; \hat\beta)\) is \[\varphi(O) = \delta(X; \beta_0) - \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} - c^\top\dot\Psi^{-1}\psi(O; \beta_0),\] and the asymptotic variance is \(\mathrm{Var}\{\varphi(O)\}\). For least squares, \(-c^\top\dot\Psi^{-1}\psi = c^\top A^{-1}Z\varepsilon\), recovering Equation 5.13. The variance splits into two components without a covariance term only under an additional orthogonality condition, for example \(\E\{\psi(O; \beta_0) \mid T, X\} = 0\); it should not be asserted for an arbitrary estimating equation.

5.3.8 What Can Go Wrong

Misspecification. If the true \(\mu(t, x)\) is nonlinear and a linear model is fitted, OLS converges to the best linear approximation, not to the \(\beta_0\) that defines \(\mu\), and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) absorbs part of the functional-form error. The bias can have either sign.

Extrapolation. Step 2 predicts \(\hat\mu(0, X_i)\) for treated units whose \(X_i\) may lie outside the support of the controls. There the model, not comparable units, supplies the answer. The overlap condition of Chapter 6 formalizes when extrapolation is unavoidable.

NoteRemark: Consistency beyond a Parametric Model

For a general estimator \(\hat\mu_t = \hat\mu(t, \cdot)\), the analogue of Equation 5.12 is \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = P_N(\hat\mu_1 - \mu_1) - P_N(\hat\mu_0 - \mu_0) + (P_N - P)(\mu_1 - \mu_0).\] The last term tends to zero by the law of large numbers. The first two tend to zero if \(P_N|\hat\mu_t - \mu_t| \pto 0\) for each \(t\), an empirical-\(L_1\) condition. The empirical form matters because \(\hat\mu\) is fitted and evaluated on the same observations: convergence in \(L_1(P_X)\) alone is not enough. The condition follows from uniform convergence, from \(L_1(P_X)\) convergence with suitable Glivenko–Cantelli or stability conditions, or from cross-fitted evaluation. It is sufficient, not necessary: if \(\hat\mu_t\) converges to a wrong limit \(\mu_t^\ast\), the estimator targets \(\E\{\mu_1^\ast(X) - \mu_0^\ast(X)\}\), which can equal \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\) even when both limits are wrong everywhere. Section 5.4 and the lab of Section 5.7 show that randomization produces exactly this cancellation for linear working models. In an observational study no design guarantees it.

Flexible learners and cross-fitting. A more flexible outcome estimator can reduce approximation bias, at the price of more estimation variability. Cross-fitting separates fitting from evaluation, but alone it guarantees neither consistency nor \(\sqrt N\) inference. Chapters 11 and 12 develop estimators whose leading error is less sensitive to errors in the outcome and propensity-score functions.

5.4 Regression Estimation under Complete Randomization

Section 5.3 justified the regression estimator \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) by a correct outcome model, with the \(N\) units treated as an i.i.d. sample. This section returns to the design-based setting of Section 5.1: the \(N\) units form a fixed finite population \(\mathcal F_N\), \(N_1\) are treated by the CRD, and the only randomness is the assignment. In this setting the difference in means is already exactly unbiased for \(\bar\tau_N\) with no model. If we fit the regression anyway, what happens when the linear model is wrong?

The answer, due to Lin (2013), is that the design, not the model, justifies \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\). It is design consistent whether or not the linear model holds, and asymptotically it cannot be less precise than \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\). The proof is the survey-sampling argument for the regression estimator, applied to each arm. Table 5.7 places this section beside Section 5.3; it is easy to carry an assumption from one framework into the other by mistake.

5.4.1 Two Regression Estimators and Three Forms

Let \(\mathbf x_i\) be the covariate vector of unit \(i\), including an intercept, and assume each arm’s design matrix has full column rank. Fit OLS separately in the two arms: \[\hat{\mathbf B}_1 = \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N T_i\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top\Bigr)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N T_i\mathbf x_iY_i, \qquad \hat{\mathbf B}_0 = \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N (1 - T_i)\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top\Bigr)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N (1 - T_i)\mathbf x_iY_i .\] The estimator of \(\bar\tau_N\) is the difference of two regression estimators, \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}}, \qquad \hat\mu_{t,\mathrm{reg}} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_i^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_t = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_t, \tag{5.16}\] which is the estimator of Section 5.3 under model (b). Write \(\mathbf x_i = (1, \mathbf u_i^\top)^\top\), so that \(\mathbf u_i\) collects the nonconstant covariates. By Proposition 5.3, \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is also the coefficient on \(T\) in the interacted regression of \(Y\) on \(\bigl(1, T, (\mathbf u - \bar{\mathbf u})^\top, T(\mathbf u - \bar{\mathbf u})^\top\bigr)\); the intercept coordinate is omitted from the centered and interaction terms, which would otherwise contain zero columns. With one covariate, \(\mathbf x_i = (1, X_i)^\top\) and \(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 = (\hat\alpha_1, \hat b_1)^\top\), \[\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\alpha_1 + \bar X\hat b_1 = \hat{\bar Y}_1 + \bigl(\bar X - \hat{\bar X}_1\bigr)\hat b_1,\] where \(\hat{\bar Y}_1\) and \(\hat{\bar X}_1\) are treated-arm means. When the treated arm has a higher covariate mean than the population and the slope is positive, the adjustment lowers the estimated mean.

Lemma 5.3 (Three Forms of the Regression Estimator) Let \(\hat e_i = Y_i - \mathbf x_i^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1\) be the fitted residuals of the treated units. Then \[\underbrace{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_i^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1}_{\text{projection form}} = \underbrace{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_i^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1 + \frac1{N_1}\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\hat e_i}_{\text{projection}\,+\,\text{expansion estimator of the residual mean}} = \underbrace{\hat{\bar Y}_1 + (\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1)^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1}_{\text{direct}\,+\,\text{augmentation}} . \tag{5.17}\]

Proof. The normal equations of the treated-arm fit are \(\sum_iT_i\mathbf x_i\hat e_i = \mathbf 0\). The first component of \(\mathbf x_i\) is \(1\), so \(\sum_iT_i\hat e_i = 0\) and the added term is zero. Expanding \(\hat e_i\) gives \(N_1^{-1}\sum_iT_i\hat e_i = \hat{\bar Y}_1 - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1\), which yields the last form. \(\square\)

The second form, projection plus the expansion (Horvitz–Thompson) estimator of the residual mean, is the generalized regression (GREG) estimator of survey sampling. Its residual term vanishes here because the OLS normal equations include an intercept and the expansion weights are constant within the arm. Under unequal-probability sampling, whether the analogous term vanishes depends on how the regression coefficients and the expansion weights are defined; when it does not, it is the term that makes GREG design consistent. The third form reads the estimator as the direct estimator \(\hat{\bar Y}_1\) corrected by an augmentation term, the covariate imbalance times the slope.

5.4.2 The Survey-Sampling Setup and the Oracle Difference Estimator

Let \(\mathcal F_N = \{(Y_i(1), Y_i(0), \mathbf x_i) : i = 1, \ldots, N\}\) be fixed. Under the CRD, the treated arm is a simple random sample (SRS) of size \(N_1\) without replacement, and the control arm is its complement. Arm 1 is therefore a survey: \(Y_i(1)\) is observed on the SRS, \(\mathbf x_i\) is known for all \(N\) units, and \(\bar{\mathbf x} = N^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf x_i\) is a known population mean. In survey terms, \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}}\) is the regression estimator of \(\bar Y(1)\). All expectations below are over the assignment, given \(\mathcal F_N\), and no model for \(Y_i(t)\) is assumed.

Definition 5.2 (Difference Estimator) For a fixed vector \(\mathbf B\), not estimated from the data, the difference estimator of \(\bar Y(1)\) is \[\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{diff}}(\mathbf B) = \hat{\bar Y}_1 + (\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1)^\top\mathbf B = \frac1{N_1}\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\bigl\{Y_i(1) - (\mathbf x_i - \bar{\mathbf x})^\top\mathbf B\bigr\}.\]

The difference estimator is an SRS mean of the fixed numbers \(u_i = Y_i(1) - (\mathbf x_i - \bar{\mathbf x})^\top\mathbf B\), whose population mean is \(\bar Y(1)\). It is therefore exactly design unbiased, with \(\mathrm{Var}\{\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{diff}}(\mathbf B) \mid \mathcal F_N\} = (N_1^{-1} - N^{-1})S_u^2\) by Theorem 5.1. The family contains the plain mean (\(\mathbf B = \mathbf 0\)). Within it, \(S_u^2\) is the variance of \(Y(1)\) around the linear function \((\mathbf x - \bar{\mathbf x})^\top\mathbf B\), which is minimized by the population OLS coefficient (Cochran 1977) \[\mathbf B_1 = \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top\Bigr)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_iY_i(1). \tag{5.18}\] The oracle difference estimator \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{diff}}(\mathbf B_1)\) is the most precise member of the family. It is not available: \(\mathbf B_1\) needs every \(Y_i(1)\). The regression estimator is the same formula with \(\mathbf B_1\) replaced by its treated-arm estimate, \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{diff}}(\hat{\mathbf B}_1)\). The oracle is a device for the proof: the next subsection shows that \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}}\) is asymptotically equivalent to it, so the design properties of the regression estimator are those of an SRS mean of residuals. Define \(\mathbf B_0\) in the same way from the \(Y_i(0)\), and the population residuals \[e_i(t) = Y_i(t) - \mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_t, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^Ne_i(t) = 0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_ie_i(t) = \mathbf 0,\] where the last two identities are the population normal equations.

5.4.3 Linearization and Design Consistency

Asymptotic statements in the design-based framework refer to a sequence of finite populations with \(N \to \infty\).

NoteRegularity Conditions (Lin 2013)
  1. The number of covariates is fixed, and \(N_1/N \to \rho \in (0, 1)\).
  2. \(N^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top\) converges to a nonsingular matrix.
  3. The fourth moments of \(Y_i(1)\), \(Y_i(0)\), and \(\mathbf x_i\) are bounded, and their first and second population moments converge.

Complete randomization alone does not give stable OLS inverses or the rates used below; the conditions do. We write \(R_N = O_p(a_N)\) if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(M < \infty\) with \(P(|R_N| > Ma_N) < \varepsilon\) for all large \(N\), and \(R_N = o_p(a_N)\) if \(R_N/a_N \pto 0\).

Lemma 5.4 (Rates for SRS Means) Under the regularity conditions, \(\hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf x} = O_p(N^{-1/2})\) and \(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 - \mathbf B_1 = O_p(N^{-1/2})\), and likewise for arm 0.

Proof. For one coordinate \(x\), the treated-arm mean is an SRS mean, so \(\E(\hat{\bar x}_1 \mid \mathcal F_N) = \bar x\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\bar x}_1 \mid \mathcal F_N) = (N_1^{-1} - N^{-1})S_x^2 \le S_x^2/N_1\). By Chebyshev’s inequality, \[P\bigl(|\hat{\bar x}_1 - \bar x| > MN^{-1/2} \bigm| \mathcal F_N\bigr) \le \frac{N}{N_1}\cdot\frac{S_x^2}{M^2},\] which is at most \(C/M^2\) for large \(N\) because \(N_1/N \to \rho > 0\) and \(S_x^2\) is bounded; choose \(M\) with \(C/M^2 < \varepsilon\). The same argument applies to every SRS mean of a variable with bounded second moment, in particular to the treated-arm means of \(\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top\) and \(\mathbf x_iY_i(1)\), whose fourth-moment bounds give bounded variances. \(\hat{\mathbf B}_1\) and \(\mathbf B_1\) are the same smooth function of these means, evaluated at SRS means and at population means respectively, and the limiting Gram matrix is nonsingular, so \(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 - \mathbf B_1 = O_p(N^{-1/2})\). \(\square\)

Theorem 5.4 (Asymptotic Equivalence and Design Consistency) Under the CRD and the regularity conditions, with no model for the potential outcomes, \[\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} = \underbrace{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_1 + \frac1{N_1}\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\bigl(Y_i - \mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_1\bigr)}_{\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{diff}}(\mathbf B_1)} + \underbrace{(\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1)^\top(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 - \mathbf B_1)}_{O_p(N^{-1})} , \tag{5.19}\] and likewise for arm 0. Consequently \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \bar\tau_N = \underbrace{\frac1{N_1}\sum_{i=1}^NT_ie_i(1) - \frac1{N_0}\sum_{i=1}^N(1 - T_i)e_i(0)}_{\mathrm{DIME}(e)} + O_p(N^{-1}), \tag{5.20}\] and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} \pto \bar\tau_N\) in the sense that \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \bar\tau_N \pto 0\).

Proof. By Lemma 5.3, adding the zero term \(N_1^{-1}\sum_iT_i(Y_i - \mathbf x_i^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1)\) to \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}}\) changes nothing. Replacing \(\hat{\mathbf B}_1\) by \(\mathbf B_1\) in both terms and collecting the difference gives Equation 5.19; the remainder is a product of two \(O_p(N^{-1/2})\) factors by Lemma 5.4. The intercept and the population normal equation give \(N^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_1 = \bar Y(1)\), and \(Y_i = Y_i(1)\) on the treated arm, so \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{diff}}(\mathbf B_1) = \bar Y(1) + N_1^{-1}\sum_iT_ie_i(1)\). Subtracting the arm-0 version gives Equation 5.20. Each \(e_i(t)\) is fixed with \(\sum_ie_i(t) = 0\), and \(\E(T_i \mid \mathcal F_N) = N_1/N\), so \(\E\{N_1^{-1}\sum_iT_ie_i(1) \mid \mathcal F_N\} = N^{-1}\sum_ie_i(1) = 0\), and its variance is \((N_1^{-1} - N^{-1})S_{e(1)}^2 = O(N^{-1})\). By Chebyshev’s inequality the term tends to zero in probability; the same holds for arm 0. \(\square\)

We write \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} = \bar\tau_N + \mathrm{DIME}(e)\) for the linearized estimator. It is the difference of two oracle difference estimators, it is exactly design unbiased, and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} = O_p(N^{-1}) = o_p(N^{-1/2})\), so the two share their first-order distribution. The model appears nowhere in the argument: \(\mathbf B_1\) is a population projection, and \(Y_i(1)\) need not be linear in \(\mathbf x_i\). Randomization justifies the estimator, not the model.

NoteRemark: Why the Remainder Vanishes Here and Not in an Observational Study

The same algebra holds in an observational study, with the expansion taken around \(\mathbf B_1^\ast = \operatorname{plim}\hat{\mathbf B}_1\): \[\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} = \frac1N\sum_i\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_1^\ast + \frac1{N_1}\sum_iT_i\bigl(Y_i - \mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_1^\ast\bigr) + (\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1)^\top(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 - \mathbf B_1^\ast).\] The difference lies in the two factors of the remainder.

5.4.4 Design Variance and Conservative Estimation

The linearized estimator is a difference in means of the fixed residuals \(e_i(1)\) and \(e_i(0)\), so Theorem 5.1 applies with \(e(t)\) in place of \(Y(t)\).

Proposition 5.5 (Design Variance of the Regression Estimator) Let \(S_{e(t)}^2\) and \(S_{e(1)-e(0)}^2\) be the finite-population variances (divisor \(N - 1\)) of the residuals and of their difference. Then \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} \mid \mathcal F_N) = \frac{S_{e(1)}^2}{N_1} + \frac{S_{e(0)}^2}{N_0} - \frac{S_{e(1)-e(0)}^2}{N} = \Bigl(\frac1{N_1} - \frac1N\Bigr)S_{e(1)}^2 + \Bigl(\frac1{N_0} - \frac1N\Bigr)S_{e(0)}^2 + \frac2NS_{e(0),e(1)} . \tag{5.21}\]

As in Neyman’s theorem, \(S_{e(1)-e(0)}^2\) is not identified, because no unit shows both residuals. Omitting its negative contribution gives the asymptotically conservative estimator \[\hat V_{\mathrm{reg}} = \frac{s_{\hat e,1}^2}{N_1} + \frac{s_{\hat e,0}^2}{N_0}, \tag{5.22}\] where \(s_{\hat e,t}^2\) is the sample variance of the fitted residuals in arm \(t\). Dropping the covariance term \(2N^{-1}S_{e(0),e(1)}\) of the second form instead would not be conservative, because that term can have either sign: if \(e_i(1) = e_i(0)\) for every unit, it is positive, and dropping it understates the variance. Under the regularity conditions, the sandwich variance of the \(T\) coefficient in the interacted regression also estimates the first-order asymptotic design variance consistently or conservatively, with the same leading \(O(N^{-1})\) term as \(\hat V_{\mathrm{reg}}\) (Lin 2013). Equation Equation 5.21 is exact for the linearized estimator; turning the distributional approximation into a statement about the actual finite-sample variance of \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) requires moment control of the remainder. The target of this inference is the finite-population \(\bar\tau_N\).

5.4.5 Regression Adjustment Cannot Increase the Asymptotic Variance

The residuals \(e_i(t)\) have smaller spread than the outcomes when \(\mathbf x\) predicts \(Y(t)\). The precise statement uses two facts about simple random sampling.

Lemma 5.5 (Covariances of SRS Means) For fixed population vectors \(u\) and \(v\) with population covariance \(S_{u,v} = (N - 1)^{-1}\sum_i(u_i - \bar u)(v_i - \bar v)\), let \(\hat{\bar u}_t\) denote the arm-\(t\) mean of \(u\). Under the CRD, \[\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\bar u}_1, \hat{\bar v}_1 \mid \mathcal F_N) = \Bigl(\frac1{N_1} - \frac1N\Bigr)S_{u,v}, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\bar u}_1, \hat{\bar v}_0 \mid \mathcal F_N) = -\frac1NS_{u,v} .\]

Proof. The first identity is the polarization of the variance calculation in the proof of Theorem 5.1, using the assignment covariances Equation 5.5. For the second, \(\sum_i(1 - T_i)v_i = \sum_iv_i - \sum_iT_iv_i\), and \(\sum_iv_i\) is fixed, so \(\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\bar u}_1, \hat{\bar v}_0) = -(N_1/N_0)\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\bar u}_1, \hat{\bar v}_1) = -(N_1/N_0)(N_1^{-1} - N^{-1})S_{u,v} = -S_{u,v}/N\). \(\square\)

Theorem 5.5 (No Loss of Precision) Under the CRD, \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_N) \;\ge\; \mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} \mid \mathcal F_N),\] an exact inequality for the linearized estimator and hence an asymptotic one for \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) under the regularity conditions. Equality holds if and only if the covariates carry no linear information about \((1 - \rho_N)Y(1) + \rho_NY(0)\), with \(\rho_N = N_1/N\).

Proof. Write \(Y_i(t) = g_i(t) + e_i(t)\) with \(g_i(t) = \mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf B_t\). Since \(\bar e(t) = 0\), \(\bar Y(t) = \bar g(t)\), and \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} - \bar\tau_N = \underbrace{\mathrm{DIME}(e)}_{=\,\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} - \bar\tau_N} + \underbrace{\bigl\{\mathrm{DIME}(g) - \bar g(1) + \bar g(0)\bigr\}}_{\text{mean zero}},\] where \(\mathrm{DIME}(g) = N_1^{-1}\sum_iT_ig_i(1) - N_0^{-1}\sum_i(1 - T_i)g_i(0)\). The population normal equations give \(\sum_ie_i(t)\mathbf x_i = \mathbf 0\) and \(\sum_ie_i(t) = 0\), so \(S_{e(t),\,g(s)} = 0\) for all \(t, s \in \{0, 1\}\). By Lemma 5.5, each of the four covariances between an arm mean of \(e(t)\) and an arm mean of \(g(s)\) is a multiple of \(S_{e(t),g(s)}\) and hence zero, so \(\mathrm{Cov}\{\mathrm{DIME}(e), \mathrm{DIME}(g) \mid \mathcal F_N\} = 0\) and \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}} \mid \mathcal F_N) = \mathrm{Var}\{\mathrm{DIME}(e) \mid \mathcal F_N\} + \mathrm{Var}\{\mathrm{DIME}(g) \mid \mathcal F_N\}.\] The second term is nonnegative. By the second form of Equation 5.3 with \(\rho_N = N_1/N\), it equals \(S^2_{(1-\rho_N)g(1) + \rho_Ng(0)}/\{N\rho_N(1 - \rho_N)\}\), which is zero if and only if \(\mathbf x^\top\{(1 - \rho_N)\mathbf B_1 + \rho_N\mathbf B_0\}\), the population projection of \((1 - \rho_N)Y(1) + \rho_NY(0)\) on \(\mathbf x\), is constant. \(\square\)

The gain is the design variance of \(\mathrm{DIME}(g)\), the part of the outcome explained by \(\mathbf x\). Adjustment buys precision, never validity; it is useless when the covariates do not predict the outcomes.

NoteRemark: Finite-Sample Bias

The difference in means is exactly design unbiased. The fitted regression estimator is not generally exactly unbiased, although it can be in special populations, for example when the potential outcomes are exactly linear in the included covariates. Theorem 5.4 gives an \(O_p(N^{-1})\) remainder, so coefficient estimation has a second-order effect on the stochastic expansion. An \(O_p\) statement controls typical size, not expectation: rare, nearly singular arm-wise fits barely affect convergence in probability but can affect the mean. With additional moment conditions that control the expectation of the remainder, the bias is \(O(N^{-1})\), while the standard error is of order \(N^{-1/2}\), so the bias is then negligible in large experiments. In small experiments, or with many covariates relative to \(N_1\) and \(N_0\), the bias and the instability of the arm-wise OLS fits can matter, and the unadjusted estimate should be reported alongside the adjusted one.

NoteRemark: Finite-Population and Superpopulation Inference

If the \(N\) units are themselves an i.i.d. sample, phase one draws \(\mathcal F_N\) and phase two the arms. Since \(\E(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} \mid \mathcal F_N) = \bar\tau_N\), \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell}) = \mathrm{Var}(\bar\tau_N) + \E\bigl\{\mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg},\ell} \mid \mathcal F_N)\bigr\}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar\tau_N) = \frac1N\mathrm{Var}\{Y(1) - Y(0)\}.\] Let \(\mathbf b_t\) be the superpopulation projection coefficients and \(r(t) = Y(t) - \mathbf x^\top\mathbf b_t\). Projection orthogonality gives \(N\,\mathrm{Var}(\bar\tau_N) = \mathrm{Var}\{\mathbf x^\top(\mathbf b_1 - \mathbf b_0)\} + \mathrm{Var}\{r(1) - r(0)\}\). The sandwich variance already omits the negative term \(-\mathrm{Var}\{r(1) - r(0)\}\) of the design variance, so for the superpopulation ATE it lacks only the variation of the fitted contrast, the sampling error of Section 5.3: \[\hat V_{\mathrm{super}} = \hat V_{\mathrm{sandwich}} + \frac1{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N\bigl\{\mathbf x_i^\top(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 - \hat{\mathbf B}_0) - \hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\bigr\}^2 ,\] a large-sample estimator under i.i.d. sampling of units and complete randomization. Adding all of \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar\tau_N)\) to the sandwich would count the residual-effect variance twice. A check: if \(Y_i(0) = 0\) and \(Y_i(1) = X_i\), then \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \bar X = \bar\tau_N\) exactly and the design variance is zero, which is correct for \(\bar\tau_N\); but estimating \(\E(X)\) from a sample still has variance \(\mathrm{Var}(X)/N\), which is the added term. Inference for \(\bar\tau_N\) conditions on the enrolled units; inference for \(\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\) also accounts for sampling them.

5.4.6 The Survey-Sampling Precedent and the Example Revisited

NoteRemark: Precedence in Survey Sampling

A completely randomized experiment is simple random sampling without replacement from the finite population of potential outcomes, so the results of this section have survey-sampling ancestors. Isaki and Fuller (1982) established the design consistency of the regression estimator under general probability designs, even when the assisting model is misspecified. Deville and Särndal (1992) showed that calibration estimators share this property, with the generalized regression estimator as the case of a quadratic distance and linear calibration weights; Section 5.5 develops this view. Lin (2013) adapted the regression estimator to the Neyman–Rubin model: the fully interacted specification cannot hurt asymptotic precision relative to \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\), and the sandwich variance is asymptotically valid or conservative under randomization.

Example 5.8 (Incentives and Services, Continued) For the 157 men of Example 5.7, Lin (2013, Table 2) reports:

Estimator Estimate Standard error
Difference in means \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\) \(-0.036\) \(0.158\)
Regression estimator \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) \(-0.081\) \(0.146\)

The standard errors are uncorrected sandwich standard errors. Both estimators target \(\bar\tau_N\), the effect for these 157 men of offering incentives in addition to services. The estimated standard error falls from 0.158 to 0.146 because the regression estimator’s variance uses residual variances; a smaller estimated standard error in one data set is consistent with, but does not by itself prove, a smaller true variance. The gain is small because high-school GPA predicts first-year GPA only weakly. The two estimates differ by 0.045, well within one standard error: the adjustment corrects a small chance imbalance in high-school GPA.

5.5 Calibration Weighting

Section 5.4 studied \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}}\) through its coefficients \(\hat{\mathbf B}_t\). This section studies the same estimator through its weights. Each arm’s linear regression estimator, fitted by least squares with an intercept, is a weighted mean of the outcomes in its arm, and the weights have two properties: they reproduce the full-sample covariate mean exactly, and among all weights that do so they are the closest to uniform. This is the calibration view of survey sampling (Deville and Särndal 1992). It explains what regression adjustment does to the covariates, and it is the bridge to stratification (Section 5.6) and to propensity-score weighting (Chapter 6).

The setting is that of Section 5.4: \(\mathbf x_i\) includes an intercept, each arm’s design matrix has full column rank, and each weight vector contains only the units of its own arm. We treat arm 1; arm 0 is identical. Write \[\hat{\mathbf M}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^NT_i\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top, \qquad \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1 = \frac1{N_1}\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\mathbf x_i, \qquad \bar{\mathbf x} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf x_i .\]

5.5.1 The Regression Estimator as a Weighted Sum

Substituting \(\hat{\mathbf B}_1 = \hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\sum_iT_i\mathbf x_iY_i\) into \(\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1\) gives a linear function of the treated outcomes, \[\hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} = \sum_{i=1}^NT_i\,\omega_{1i}Y_i, \qquad \omega_{1i} = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\mathbf x_i , \tag{5.23}\] and likewise \(\hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}} = \sum_i(1 - T_i)\,\omega_{0i}Y_i\) with \(\omega_{0i} = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\{\sum_j(1 - T_j)\mathbf x_j\mathbf x_j^\top\}^{-1}\mathbf x_i\). The weights depend on \(\mathbf x\) and \(T\) only, not on \(Y\), so they can be computed and inspected before any outcome is seen. With \(\mathbf x_i = 1\) alone, they are \(1/N_1\) and \(1/N_0\), and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\).

Proposition 5.6 (The Regression Weights Satisfy the Calibration Constraint) \[\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\,\omega_{1i}\mathbf x_i = \bar{\mathbf x}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^N(1 - T_i)\,\omega_{0i}\mathbf x_i = \bar{\mathbf x}. \tag{5.24}\]

Proof. \(\sum_iT_i\omega_{1i}\mathbf x_i = \sum_iT_i\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\bar{\mathbf x} = \hat{\mathbf M}_1\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\bar{\mathbf x} = \bar{\mathbf x}\); the control arm is the same. \(\square\)

Each weighted arm reproduces the full-sample covariate mean exactly. The intercept is one of the covariates, so the weights sum to one. Condition Equation 5.24 is called the covariate-balancing or calibration constraint. Balance of these means does not by itself establish exchangeability: it concerns only the covariate functions included in \(\mathbf x\). Many weight vectors satisfy Equation 5.24 when \(N_1 > p\). The rest of this section asks which one the regression estimator uses, and why.

5.5.2 Which Weights? A Working Model

Consider all weighted estimators \(\hat\mu_{\boldsymbol\omega} = \sum_iT_i\omega_iY_i\) of \(\bar Y(1)\) with normalized weights, \(\sum_iT_i\omega_i = 1\); the plain mean has \(\omega_i = 1/N_1\). To compare them, adopt the working model \[Y_i(1) = \mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf T) = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf T) = \sigma^2\mathbf I_N ,\] and define the calibration residual \(\mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega) = \bar{\mathbf x} - \sum_iT_i\omega_i\mathbf x_i\), the target minus the achieved covariate mean. For fixed weights, \[\hat\mu_{\boldsymbol\omega} - \bar Y(1) = -\mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega)^\top\boldsymbol\beta + \sum_{i=1}^N\Bigl(T_i\omega_i - \frac1N\Bigr)\varepsilon_i ,\] because the target \(\bar Y(1)\) contains the same errors as the estimator. Hence, for normalized weights, \[\mathrm{MSE}\bigl\{\hat\mu_{\boldsymbol\omega} - \bar Y(1) \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf T\bigr\} = \bigl\{\mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega)^\top\boldsymbol\beta\bigr\}^2 + \sigma^2\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\omega_i^2 - \frac1N\Bigr), \tag{5.25}\] since \(\sum_i(T_i\omega_i - N^{-1})^2 = \sum_iT_i\omega_i^2 - 2N^{-1}\sum_iT_i\omega_i + N^{-1}\). The model supplies the three ingredients of the optimization.

  • The constraint. \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is unknown. The estimator is model-unbiased for every \(\boldsymbol\beta\) if and only if \(\mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega) = \mathbf 0\): the weighted covariate mean must equal the known \(\bar{\mathbf x}\).
  • The distance. With the bias removed, minimizing the MSE means minimizing \(\sum_iT_i\omega_i^2\).
  • The anchor. On the normalized set, \(\sum_iT_i\omega_i^2 = \sum_iT_i(\omega_i - 1/N_1)^2 + 1/N_1\), so the criterion is the Euclidean distance to the anchor, the initial weights \(1/N_1\).

Only the form of the model is used; \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is never estimated. The model motivates the criterion; the identity between calibration and OLS in the next subsection does not need it to be true, and the design-based results of Section 5.4 do not use it at all.

5.5.3 \(L_2\) Calibration and the Lagrange Multiplier Method

The calibration problem for arm 1 is \[\min_{\boldsymbol\omega}\; D(\boldsymbol\omega) = \frac12\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\Bigl(\omega_i - \frac1{N_1}\Bigr)^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega) = \bar{\mathbf x} - \sum_{i=1}^NT_i\omega_i\mathbf x_i = \mathbf 0, \tag{5.26}\] with Lagrangian \(\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\omega, \boldsymbol\lambda) = D(\boldsymbol\omega) + \boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega)\) and multiplier \(\boldsymbol\lambda \in \mathbb R^p\). The multiplier method turns a problem in \(N_1\) unknowns into one in \(p\) unknowns.

  1. Minimize in \(\boldsymbol\omega\) for fixed \(\boldsymbol\lambda\). Setting \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\omega_i = 0\) gives, unit by unit, the linear weight form \[\omega_i^\star(\boldsymbol\lambda) = \frac1{N_1} + \boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf x_i :\] the anchor plus a linear function of \(\mathbf x_i\), with the same \(p\) coefficients for every unit.
  2. Maximize the dual. Substituting, \(D(\boldsymbol\omega^\star) = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1\boldsymbol\lambda\) and \(\mathbf h(\boldsymbol\omega^\star) = \bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1 - \hat{\mathbf M}_1\boldsymbol\lambda\), so the dual function is the concave quadratic \[\ell(\boldsymbol\lambda) = \mathcal L\{\boldsymbol\omega^\star(\boldsymbol\lambda), \boldsymbol\lambda\} = \boldsymbol\lambda^\top(\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1) - \frac12\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1\boldsymbol\lambda ,\] whose gradient is the calibration residual \(\mathbf h\{\boldsymbol\omega^\star(\boldsymbol\lambda)\}\).
  3. Return. \(\nabla\ell = \mathbf 0\) gives \(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda} = \hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1)\) and the regression weights \[\hat\omega_i = \frac1{N_1} + (\bar{\mathbf x} - \hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1)^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\mathbf x_i = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\mathbf x_i . \tag{5.27}\] The second form holds because the first column of \(\hat{\mathbf M}_1\) is \(\sum_iT_i\mathbf x_i = N_1\hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1\), so \(\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1 = \mathbf e_1/N_1\) and \(\hat{\bar{\mathbf x}}_1^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\mathbf x_i = 1/N_1\).

That the two steps return the solution of Equation 5.26 is proved in Section 5.5.4.

Theorem 5.6 (Calibration Equals Regression (Fuller 1975; Deville and Särndal 1992)) The solution of Equation 5.26 is \(\hat\omega_i = \omega_{1i}\) of Equation 5.23, and for every outcome variable \(Y\), \[\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\,\hat\omega_iY_i = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^NT_i\mathbf x_iY_i = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1 = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} .\]

The identity is exact, not asymptotic. The \(N_1\)-dimensional weight problem and the \(p\)-dimensional least-squares problem compute the same number. The weights do not depend on \(Y\), and the coefficient does: calibration separates the work on \(\mathbf x\), done once, from the work on \(Y\), done for each outcome. In an experiment with many outcomes, one set of weights gives the regression estimator for each, and the design consistency of Section 5.4 carries over to the weighted form. Nothing in the problem prevents \(\hat\omega_i < 0\).

5.5.4 KKT Conditions and Strong Duality

The multiplier method is an instance of a general characterization. Write \(n = N_1\) for the size of arm 1, let \(\mathbf X_1\) be the \(n \times p\) matrix of treated covariate rows, and consider the calibration set \[\mathcal C_\omega = \{\boldsymbol\omega \in \mathbb R^n : \mathbf X_1^\top\boldsymbol\omega = \bar{\mathbf x}\}.\] Two subspaces of \(\mathbb R^n\) organize the argument: \[\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1) = \{\mathbf X_1\mathbf a : \mathbf a \in \mathbb R^p\}, \qquad \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top) = \{\mathbf k \in \mathbb R^n : \mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf k = \mathbf 0\}.\] \(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\), the null space of \(\mathbf X_1^\top\), contains the directions that leave every weighted \(\mathbf x\)-mean unchanged. \(\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\) contains the vectors whose \(i\)th entry is a linear function \(\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf a\) of the covariates.

Lemma 5.6 (Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra) \(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top) = \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)^\perp\), so \(\mathbb R^n = \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1) \oplus \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\) with the two subspaces orthogonal.

Proof. \(\mathbf k \in \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\) if and only if \(\mathbf k^\top\mathbf X_1\mathbf a = (\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf k)^\top\mathbf a = 0\) for every \(\mathbf a\), that is, if and only if \(\mathbf k \perp \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\). Every subspace \(\mathcal S\) of \(\mathbb R^n\) gives \(\mathbb R^n = \mathcal S \oplus \mathcal S^\perp\). \(\square\)

Proposition 5.7 (KKT Conditions for Calibration) Let \(D : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R\) be convex and differentiable, and let \(\mathcal C_\omega\) be nonempty. Then \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\) minimizes \(D\) over \(\mathcal C_\omega\) if and only if, for some \(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda} \in \mathbb R^p\),

  1. feasibility: \(\mathbf X_1^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} = \bar{\mathbf x}\);

  2. stationarity: \(\nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) = \mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}\), that is, \(\partial D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})/\partial\omega_i = \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}^\top\mathbf x_i\) for every treated unit.

If \(D\) is strictly convex, the minimizer is unique; if \(\mathbf X_1\) has full column rank, the multiplier is unique.

Proof. Feasible directions. For \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} \in \mathcal C_\omega\), the line \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} + t\mathbf k\) stays in \(\mathcal C_\omega\) for all \(t \in \mathbb R\) if and only if \(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf k = \mathbf 0\). The feasible directions are exactly \(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\), each in both senses.

Necessity. Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\) minimize \(D\) over \(\mathcal C_\omega\) and let \(\mathbf k \in \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\). The function \(g(t) = D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} + t\mathbf k)\) is differentiable and minimized at \(t = 0\) over all of \(\mathbb R\), so \(0 = g'(0) = \nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})^\top\mathbf k\). This holds for every \(\mathbf k \in \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\), so \(\nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) \in \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)^\perp = \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\) by Lemma 5.6.

Sufficiency. Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} \in \mathcal C_\omega\) satisfy (ii), and let \(\boldsymbol\omega \in \mathcal C_\omega\). Then \(\boldsymbol\omega - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega} \in \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\) and \(\nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) \in \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\), so \(\nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})^\top(\boldsymbol\omega - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) = 0\). Convexity gives \(D(\boldsymbol\omega) \ge D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) + \nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})^\top(\boldsymbol\omega - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) = D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})\).

Uniqueness. If \(D\) is strictly convex, two minimizers would give a strictly smaller value at their midpoint, which is feasible. If \(\mathbf X_1\) has full column rank, \(\mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda} = \mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}'\) implies \(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda} = \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}'\). \(\square\)

With equality constraints only, the KKT (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker) conditions are (i) and (ii); together they say \(\nabla\mathcal L(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}, \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}) = \mathbf 0\), in \(\boldsymbol\omega\) (stationarity) and in \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) (feasibility, \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\lambda}\mathcal L = \mathbf h\)). For the Euclidean distance, \(\nabla D(\boldsymbol\omega) = \boldsymbol\omega - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)}\) with anchor \(\boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} = n^{-1}\mathbf 1\), and stationarity is the linear weight form \(\boldsymbol\omega = \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} + \mathbf X_1\boldsymbol\lambda\) of Section 5.5.3. For a general \(D\) it is the gradient, not the weights, that is linear in \(\mathbf x_i\); a nonlinear \(\nabla D\) gives nonlinear weights.

Proposition 5.8 (Strong Duality) Assume \(D\) is convex and differentiable on \(\mathbb R^n\), the constraints are affine, and the primal problem has a minimizer \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\). With \(\ell(\boldsymbol\lambda) = \inf_{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\omega, \boldsymbol\lambda)\), \[\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda}\ell(\boldsymbol\lambda) = \ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}) = D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) = \min_{\boldsymbol\omega}\sup_{\boldsymbol\lambda}\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\omega, \boldsymbol\lambda),\] with \(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}\) the multiplier of Proposition 5.7.

Proof. By Proposition 5.7, \(\nabla D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) = \mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}\), so \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\omega}\mathcal L(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}, \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}) = \mathbf 0\). The function \(\mathcal L(\cdot, \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}) = D + \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}^\top\mathbf h\) is convex, so its stationary point \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\) is its global minimizer: step 1 of the multiplier method returns \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\) at \(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}\). Since \(\mathbf h(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) = \mathbf 0\), \(\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}) = \mathcal L(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}, \hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}) = D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})\). For any \(\boldsymbol\lambda\), weak duality gives \(\ell(\boldsymbol\lambda) \le \mathcal L(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}, \boldsymbol\lambda) = D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda}\) maximizes \(\ell\). Finally, \(\sup_{\boldsymbol\lambda}\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\omega, \boldsymbol\lambda)\) equals \(D(\boldsymbol\omega)\) on \(\mathcal C_\omega\) and \(+\infty\) off it, so its minimum is \(D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega})\). \(\square\)

Strong duality justifies steps 2 and 3 of the multiplier method: maximizing the \(p\)-dimensional dual returns the multiplier of the primal solution, and the linear weight form at that multiplier is the calibrated weight vector.

5.5.5 Geometry: Orthogonal Projection

For the Euclidean distance the solution has a geometric reading. \(\mathcal C_\omega\) is an affine set, \(\mathcal C_\omega = \boldsymbol\omega_1 + \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\) for any \(\boldsymbol\omega_1 \in \mathcal C_\omega\), and the level sets of \(D(\boldsymbol\omega) = \tfrac12\|\boldsymbol\omega - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)}\|^2\) are spheres around the anchor. Growing the spheres until one touches \(\mathcal C_\omega\) gives the solution. At the touching point, moving along \(\mathcal C_\omega\) cannot lower \(D\) to first order, so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} \perp \mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\), that is, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} \in \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\): the linear weight form, read as a picture.

Proposition 5.9 (Pythagorean Identity) For every \(\boldsymbol\omega_1 \in \mathcal C_\omega\), \[D(\boldsymbol\omega_1) = D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) + \tfrac12\|\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\|^2 \;\ge\; D(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}),\] with equality if and only if \(\boldsymbol\omega_1 = \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\). Hence the \(L_2\) calibration problem has a unique solution.

Proof. \(\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} = (\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}) + (\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)})\). The first vector lies in \(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\) because both \(\boldsymbol\omega_1\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\) are calibrated; the second lies in \(\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\) by the linear weight form. By Lemma 5.6 they are orthogonal, so the cross term vanishes. \(\square\)

The argument proves optimality without multipliers. It is the same right-triangle argument as the analysis-of-variance decomposition in regression.

The two-projection formula. Let \(\mathbf P_1 = \mathbf X_1(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf X_1)^{-1}\mathbf X_1^\top\), the hat matrix of the regression on \(\mathbf X_1\) in arm 1. For any \(\boldsymbol\omega_1 \in \mathcal C_\omega\), \(\mathbf X_1^\top(\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)}) = \bar{\mathbf x} - \mathbf X_1^\top\boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} = \mathbf X_1^\top(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)})\), and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} = \mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\lambda} \in \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} = \mathbf P_1(\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \boldsymbol\omega^{(0)})\). Rearranging, \[\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} = (\mathbf I - \mathbf P_1)\boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} + \mathbf P_1\boldsymbol\omega_1 . \tag{5.28}\] The first term is the projection of the anchor onto \(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\); the second is the projection of any calibrated vector onto \(\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\). The anchor matters only through its residual. Here \(\mathbf x_i\) contains an intercept, so the uniform anchor \(\boldsymbol\omega^{(0)} = N_1^{-1}\mathbf 1\) lies in \(\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\), the first term vanishes, and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} = \mathbf P_1\boldsymbol\omega_1\) for every calibrated \(\boldsymbol\omega_1\). Since \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} \in \mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\) and every calibrated vector differs from it by an element of \(\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf X_1^\top)\), \(\|\boldsymbol\omega_1\|^2 = \|\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\|^2 + \|\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\|^2\): the regression weights are the minimum-norm calibrated weights, which links the geometry back to the variance criterion of Section 5.5.2. In survey sampling the anchor is a vector \(\mathbf d\) of design weights, which is usually not in \(\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf X_1)\). For the unscaled Euclidean distance around \(\mathbf d\), the calibrated weights are \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega} = \mathbf d + \mathbf X_1(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf X_1)^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf x} - \mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf d)\), so \[\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}^\top\mathbf y = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1 + \mathbf d^\top(\mathbf y - \mathbf X_1\hat{\mathbf B}_1).\] With a general design-weight anchor, the calibrated estimator therefore retains an anchor-weighted residual term. It has the generalized regression form and need not equal the pure OLS prediction \(\bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1\).

NoteRemark: Calibration Equals Regression, a Second Proof

\(\mathbf P_1\) is symmetric. For any calibrated \(\boldsymbol\omega_1\) and any outcome vector \(\mathbf y\) of the treated units, \[\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}^\top\mathbf y = (\mathbf P_1\boldsymbol\omega_1)^\top\mathbf y = \boldsymbol\omega_1^\top\mathbf P_1\mathbf y = \boldsymbol\omega_1^\top\mathbf X_1\hat{\mathbf B}_1 = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf B}_1 .\] One matrix links the two constructions: \(\mathbf P_1\) maps \(\mathbf y\) to its fitted values and any calibrated weight vector to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\omega}\). The coefficient on the \(\mathbf y\) side, \(\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf y\), depends on the outcome; the coefficient on the weight side, \(\hat{\mathbf M}_1^{-1}\bar{\mathbf x}\), depends on the benchmark only.

5.6 Stratification

Stratification (subclassification) divides the units into strata of similar covariate values and compares treated and control units within each stratum. It is the model-free implementation of the back-door formula, and it is also a special case of calibration.

5.6.1 Calibration on Stratum Indicators

Let the strata be \(\mathcal S_k = \{i : X_i \in B_k\}\), \(k = 1, \ldots, K\), with \(N_k\) units in stratum \(k\) and \(N_{tk}\) units of arm \(t\) in stratum \(k\). Take the covariate vector of Section 5.5 to be the stratum indicators, \(\mathbf x_i = (x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{iK})^\top\) with \(x_{ik} = \mathbb{I}(i \in \mathcal S_k)\). This is a full-rank coding with no extra intercept; the indicators sum to one, so the intercept is in their span.

Proposition 5.10 (Stratification Is Calibration on Stratum Indicators) Assume \(N_{tk} \ge 1\) for every \(t\) and \(k\). With stratum indicators as covariates:

  1. the calibration constraint Equation 5.24 for arm \(t\) says \(\sum_{i:\,T_i=t,\,i\in\mathcal S_k}\omega_{ti} = N_k/N\) for every \(k\): each stratum receives its population share;
  2. the \(L_2\)-calibrated weights are equal within each arm-by-stratum cell, \(\hat\omega_{ti} = N_k/(N\,N_{tk})\) for \(i \in \mathcal S_k\);
  3. the resulting regression estimators are \[\hat\mu_{t,\mathrm{reg}} = \sum_{k=1}^K\frac{N_k}{N}\,\bar Y_{t,k}, \qquad \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\tau_{\mathrm{STRAT}},\] where \(\bar Y_{t,k}\) is the mean of \(Y\) among arm-\(t\) units in stratum \(k\) and \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{STRAT}}\) is defined in Equation 5.29 below.

Proof. (i) The \(k\)th coordinate of \(\sum_iT_i\omega_{1i}\mathbf x_i\) is the total weight of the treated units in stratum \(k\), and the \(k\)th coordinate of \(\bar{\mathbf x}\) is \(N_k/N\). (ii) \(\hat{\mathbf M}_t = \mathrm{diag}(N_{t1}, \ldots, N_{tK})\), which is invertible because every \(N_{tk} \ge 1\), so by Equation 5.27 \(\hat\omega_{ti} = \bar{\mathbf x}^\top\hat{\mathbf M}_t^{-1}\mathbf x_i = N_k/(NN_{tk})\) for \(i \in \mathcal S_k\). (iii) Summing \(\hat\omega_{ti}Y_i\) over the arm-\(t\) units of stratum \(k\) gives \((N_k/N)\bar Y_{t,k}\). \(\square\)

The full-rank condition \(N_{tk} \ge 1\) is the within-stratum overlap requirement: a stratum with no treated or no control units cannot be calibrated. In a completely randomized experiment the same estimator is the post-stratified difference in means, which is regression on stratum indicators. For a fixed number of strata whose proportions satisfy the regularity conditions of Section 5.4, it is design consistent and has no larger first-order asymptotic variance than \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DIM}}\). The finite-sample condition \(N_{tk} \ge 1\) alone is not such a condition, and progressively finer strata, with a growing number of indicators, fall outside that result.

5.6.2 The Stratified Estimator and Its Conditions

Within stratum \(k\), the difference in means is \(\hat\tau_k = \bar Y_{1,k} - \bar Y_{0,k}\), and the stratified estimator averages the within-stratum contrasts with the population shares, \[\hat\tau_{\mathrm{STRAT}} = \sum_{k=1}^K\hat\tau_k\,\frac{N_k}{N}. \tag{5.29}\] For categorical \(X\), with one stratum per category, it is the standardization estimator Equation 5.10. For continuous \(X\), the strata coarsen \(X\), and coarsening does not by itself inherit exchangeability: residual confounding can persist within every finite-width stratum. What justifies the method in an observational study is a combination of conditions:

  • exchangeability given the exact \(X\);
  • treatment propensity and outcome regressions that vary smoothly within strata;
  • stratum widths that shrink as \(N\) grows;
  • adequate within-stratum overlap, with growing numbers of treated and control units within strata.

Under these conditions the coarsening bias of \(\hat\tau_k\) vanishes, and \(\hat\tau_k\) is consistent for the stratum-specific effect. With finite widths, coarsening can leave residual confounding within a stratum, so finite-width strata do not generally give unbiased comparisons. Exact categorical strata, or strata within which treatment is randomized, are the exceptions, provided both arms are represented. The last condition matters on its own: a stratum with one unit from each arm has overlap but highly variable cell means.

Cochran (1968) studied one continuous confounder under particular distributional models. With \(K = 5\) strata of equal size, about 90% of the bias is removed; with \(K = 10\), over 95%. This is a benchmark, not a universal fact. More strata are not always better: with too many, empty cells and within-stratum variance dominate, and the mean squared error grows.

NoteRemark: Stratification, Standardization, and Survey-Sampling Terminology

For categorical \(X\) the stratified and standardization estimators are identical. For continuous \(X\), stratification fits a step function in \(x\), which approaches a nonparametric regression target as the strata shrink and the cell counts grow.

In survey sampling, stratification is a design-stage decision: the population is divided into strata before sampling, and units are sampled independently within each stratum to control the allocation. Post-stratification is an analysis-stage adjustment: weights are adjusted after sampling so that weighted stratum proportions match specified population proportions. Stratum membership may be known before sampling; it is simply not used to set the allocation. The stratified estimator Equation 5.29 is the analysis-stage operation.

The weights deserve one more distinction. With \(\hat p_k = N_k/N\), the empirical shares, \(\sum_k\hat p_k\hat\tau_k\) estimates the effect for the study units. With \(q_k\), the stratum shares of a different target population, \(\sum_kq_k\hat\tau_k\) targets that population. The second interpretation requires the stratum-specific effects to apply to the target population and every target stratum to be represented in the data; changing the weights alone does not establish either.

5.6.3 Three Implementations of One Formula

Regression adjustment, standardization, and stratification implement the same identification formula Equation 5.7 and rest on the same identification conditions: consistency, conditional exchangeability, and positivity. They differ in how they handle \(X\) at the estimation stage (Table 5.8).

All three face the curse of dimensionality. With \(p\) binary covariates there are \(2^p\) cells, most of them sparse or empty. Regression interpolates across sparse cells at the price of a model. The propensity score reduces the dimension needed for adjustment to one (Chapter 6), although estimating it from a high-dimensional \(X\) can still be difficult. None of the three repairs unmeasured confounding.

5.7 Lab: Simulation Study of the Outcome Regression Estimator

The identification results of Section 5.2 determine the target before any outcome model is chosen. Estimating it by the regression estimator of Section 5.3 then creates a bias–variance tradeoff: a restrictive working model may be biased, while a flexible estimator pays in variance. Under complete randomization, Theorem 5.4 gives the arm-wise linear regression estimator a design-consistency guarantee even when its working model is wrong; the guarantee disappears when treatment is confounded. This simulation puts the two claims side by side.

NoteDGP for the Lab

Let \(X \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 1)\) and \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\) independent of \(X\), and \[Y(t) = t + 3(1 + t)X^2 + \varepsilon, \qquad t \in \{0, 1\}.\] The conditional average effect is \(\tau(x) = 1 + 3x^2\), so \[\tau_{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E(1 + 3X^2) = 2.\] Two assignment mechanisms are studied.

  • CRD: exactly \(N/2\) units are drawn without replacement for treatment, independently of \(X\) and the potential outcomes.
  • Observational: \(T \mid X \sim \mathrm{Bern}\{\expit(-2 + 5X)\}\), so \(\pi(0) \approx 0.12\), \(\pi(0.5) \approx 0.62\), and \(\pi(1) \approx 0.95\): high-\(X\) units are nearly always treated.

The observed outcome is \(Y = Y(T)\). Strong ignorability holds in both designs: in the CRD because assignment is independent of \((Y(0), Y(1), X)\), and in the observational design because assignment depends on \(X\) alone.

Estimators. Both estimators follow the three-step recipe of Section 5.3.3: estimate \(\hat\mu(t, x)\) within each arm, predict both conditional means at every observed \(X_i\), and average, \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = N^{-1}\sum_i\{\hat\mu(1, X_i) - \hat\mu(0, X_i)\}\). They differ only in how \(\hat\mu\) is estimated.

  • Linear OR. Within each arm, fit \(Y\) on \((1, X)\) by OLS. The model is misspecified: the true \(\mu(t, x) = t + 3(1 + t)x^2\) has a quadratic term and a treatment–covariate interaction.
  • Local-linear OR. Within each arm, local linear regression with a Gaussian kernel and the prescribed bandwidth rule \(h = 0.5\,N^{-1/5}\) (KernSmooth::locpoly in R), computed on a 401-point grid covering the whole target range \([0, 1]\); predictions at the observed \(X_i\) are obtained by linear interpolation of the fitted curve (approx). Local linear regression removes the leading boundary bias of the Nadaraya–Watson estimator, which matters here because the observational design places treated and control units in different regions of \([0, 1]\).

Three lessons stand out (Table 5.9).

1. Under the CRD, the misspecified linear estimator is consistent. Conditional on each simulated finite population, Theorem 5.4 gives \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} - \bar\tau_N \pto 0\) under the randomization distribution. Because the units are first drawn i.i.d. from the superpopulation, \(\bar\tau_N \pto 2\), and the two phases together give \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} \pto \tau_{\mathrm{ATE}}\). Randomization, not the model, supplies the guarantee.

2. Under the observational design, the same estimator is biased. The estimated absolute bias increases from \(0.0036\) to \(0.0326\). The model and the data-generating process are the same; only the assignment mechanism differs. The two estimates carry different weight. Under the CRD, \(-0.0036\) is about two Monte Carlo standard errors from zero and is consistent with the \(O(N^{-1})\) finite-sample behavior described above. Under the observational design, numerical integration of the population arm-wise fits gives a probability limit of about \(1.9652\), a limiting bias of about \(-0.0348\), which the simulation estimate matches. A single table at \(N = 1{,}000\) illustrates finite-sample performance; consistency follows from the theory, not from one sample size.

3. Flexibility trades bias for variance. Under the observational design the local-linear estimator reduces the absolute bias from \(0.0326\) to \(0.0273\), but its standard deviation is \(0.0951\) against \(0.0895\), and its RMSE is larger (\(0.0989\) against \(0.0952\)). In this finite-sample comparison the parsimonious, misspecified model has the smaller mean squared error.

WarningModel Misspecification and Confounding Interact

The cancellation under the CRD can be seen exactly. Under the CRD, \(X \mid T = t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 1)\) in the lab’s superpopulation. The population OLS line of \(Y(t)\) on \(X\) has slope \(3(1 + t)\,\mathrm{Cov}(X^2, X)/\mathrm{Var}(X) = 3(1 + t)\), because both moments equal \(1/12\). The two lines are \(\mu_{\mathrm{lin}}(0, x) = -\tfrac12 + 3x\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{lin}}(1, x) = 6x\), so the pointwise error of the fitted contrast is \[b(x) = \bigl\{\mu_{\mathrm{lin}}(1, x) - \mu_{\mathrm{lin}}(0, x)\bigr\} - \tau(x) = -\tfrac12 + 3x - 3x^2 .\] It is zero only at \(x = (3 \mp \sqrt3)/6 \approx 0.21\) and \(0.79\), yet \(\int_0^1b(x)\,dx = -\tfrac12 + \tfrac32 - 1 = 0\). The cancellation is structural. The difference of the two population OLS fits is the \(L_2\) projection of \(\tau(X)\) onto \(\mathrm{span}\{1, X\}\) under the fitting distribution, and a projection onto a space with an intercept preserves the mean. Under the CRD the fitting distribution equals the evaluation distribution, so the errors cancel. Under the observational design the arm-1 line is fitted on high \(X\) and the arm-0 line on low \(X\), both are evaluated on the marginal of \(X\), and the errors no longer cancel.

The conclusion is narrow. The misspecified model is biased for \(\tau(x)\) at almost every \(x\), gives a misleading picture of heterogeneity, and need not give correct model-based standard errors; this is why sandwich variance estimators accompany regression adjustment in experiments (Section 5.4.4). Integrated errors can also cancel in particular confounded designs, so the correspondence between confounding and bias is typical, not exact.

NoteRemark: What This Lab Does Not Show

The bandwidth follows a prescribed rule. A data-driven bandwidth, for example the direct plug-in selector KernSmooth::dpill, changes the bias–variance tradeoff and may change the local-linear RMSE. The lab studies a scalar \(X\) only; with multivariate \(X\), local linear regression faces the curse of dimensionality, one motivation for the propensity score of Chapter 6.

5.8 Where the Propensity Score Fits

Section 5.6.3 placed regression, standardization, and stratification as three implementations of one formula, and the calibration view of Section 5.5 showed what they share: each reweights the arms so that they look alike on the covariates. Regression does it through calibrated weights, stratification through cells, and the propensity score through a model for treatment. When \(X\) has many components, the propensity score provides an identification-preserving dimension reduction: adjusting for the scalar \(\pi(X)\) suffices for identification even when \(X\) is high-dimensional. The theorem reduces the dimension of the balancing variable, not every statistical difficulty.

NoteBridge to Chapter 6

Chapter 6 proves the balancing property \(T \indep X \mid \pi(X)\) and the Propensity Score Theorem, then develops matching on the score and inverse probability weighting, in Horvitz–Thompson and Hájek forms, with balance and overlap diagnostics. True inverse-probability weights balance the covariates in the population; ordinary estimated inverse-probability weights generally give only approximate sample balance, which must be checked, although some procedures choose the weights or fit the score to impose selected balance equations exactly. Propensity-score subclassification is the scalar-score analogue of the stratification of Section 5.6. Matching need not use the score at all: the same identification assumptions justify the target of Mahalanobis-distance matching on \(X\), although the validity of the estimator still requires conditions that control matching discrepancies and overlap.

5.9 Summary

  1. Randomization. A known assignment mechanism supplies both identification and the probability distribution for inference. Under complete randomization the difference in means is exactly unbiased for the finite-population ATE \(\bar\tau_N\), its design variance is given by Neyman’s formula, and the Neyman variance estimator is conservative because the variance of the unit-level effects is not identified. Fisher’s randomization test gives an exact test of the sharp null. Extending conclusions to a superpopulation needs a sampling or transportability argument.

  2. Ignorability. In an observational study, consistency, weak conditional exchangeability, and positivity identify the ATE and the ATT through the back-door formula. The assumption is substantive, cannot be verified from the observed law, and requires pretreatment adjustment variables.

  3. Outcome regression. The regression estimator \(\hat\tau_{\mathrm{reg}} = \hat\mu_{1,\mathrm{reg}} - \hat\mu_{0,\mathrm{reg}}\) plugs a fitted outcome model and the empirical covariate distribution into the identified functional. Under a correct linear model its error splits into an estimation error, from the nuisance parameter \(\hat\beta\), and a sampling error, from averaging over the sampled covariates. Their first-order influence-function components are uncorrelated, and the influence function gives the asymptotic variance.

  4. Regression under randomization. Under complete randomization the same estimator is asymptotically equivalent to an oracle difference estimator, an SRS mean of fixed residuals. Exact results hold for the oracle (linearized) estimator: its variance is Neyman’s formula with residuals, and it is never less precise than the difference in means. To first order the fitted estimator inherits these properties: it is design consistent without any outcome model and cannot increase the asymptotic variance. The remainder of the linearization is negligible because randomization balances \(\mathbf x\). In an observational study it can be first order, and correct specification of the outcome model is a sufficient, not a necessary, condition for consistency.

  5. Calibration. The arm-wise linear regression estimator studied here, least squares with an intercept, is a weighted mean whose weights reproduce the full-sample covariate mean exactly and, among all such weights, are the closest to uniform. For this estimator, Euclidean calibration equals regression for every outcome; balancing the included covariate means does not by itself establish exchangeability; the KKT conditions characterize optimal calibration weights for any convex distance, and for the Euclidean distance the solution is an orthogonal projection.

  6. Stratification and the propensity score. Stratification is calibration on stratum indicators. Regression, standardization, and stratification implement one identification formula and differ only in how they handle \(X\); the propensity score of Chapter 6 reduces \(X\) to a scalar. None of them repairs unmeasured confounding.
